Idea: The larger a coin's fall on its latest day, measured against the size of its own daily moves over the past 60 days, the more it rises over the next 2 days, and the more so the larger its daily moves over the past 10 days are against those of the past 60 days.
Consequence: A trader sizes a two-day rebound trade after a sharp daily drop by both the drop and how turbulent the coin's recent days have been, putting little or nothing on drops after a calm stretch.
Why:
- F2: Turbulence decides whether the rebound appears. Turbulence here means the coin's daily moves over the past 10 days against its past 60 days, split in quintiles. In the calmest quintile, falls are followed by a 2-day excess return of -0.76 % (t -3.41, 705 cases), and its falls above one usual move by -2.19 % (51 cases). In the two most turbulent quintiles the same falls are followed by +0.53 % (t 2.07, 739 cases) and +0.68 % (t 2.29, 721 cases), and falls above one usual move by +0.68 % and +1.60 %.
- F3: The turbulence condition is not an effect of its own. In the most turbulent third, bars without a fall have a 2-day excess return of -0.20 % (t -0.94, 1186 cases), against +0.65 % (t 2.92, 1218 cases) where a fall and turbulence occur together. The joint case gives +0.65 % on 516 distinct days (day-clustered t 1.79) and is positive in 6 of the 8 years. This belongs to the idea's own theory: a sharp drop amid stretched, one-sided flow is the liquidity shock that market makers are paid to absorb.
- F5: Small falls do not rebound. Falls under half a usual move give a 2-day excess return of -0.43 % (1694 cases), while falls of 0.5 to 1, 1 to 1.5, 1.5 to 2, 2 to 3 and over 3 usual moves give +0.48 %, +0.88 %, +0.63 %, +0.68 % and +0.59 %. The step from small to medium falls carries most of the grading.
