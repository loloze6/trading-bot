The operator asks whether Bitcoin's moves lead those of smaller coins. A relation between two coins cannot be tested on one coin's bars, and that claim is already in the index. This brief tests the mechanism usually given for that lead, and that mechanism should leave a trace in a small coin's own prices.

The usual reason for the lead is that thin coins absorb new information slowly. Market makers in thin books update their quotes late and cautiously. Many holders trade by hand and only rarely. Arbitrage capital is scarce, so nobody is forced to close the gap quickly. When news reaches the whole market, the price of a small coin then adjusts in steps rather than all at once.

If that is true, it does not need Bitcoin to show. A small coin's own short-horizon returns should continue in the direction of the move just made. Each partial adjustment is followed by more of the same, so the price shows positive serial dependence over short spans. This continuation should be weaker or absent in the deepest, most actively quoted coins. It should fade over longer spans, once the adjustment is complete.

The reasoning is mine, not from a source. It rests on the assumption that slow quote updating, not some other cause, drives the lead.

The theory is wrong if a small coin's short-horizon returns show no continuation. It is also wrong if they tend to reverse instead, which would point to liquidity providers being paid for noise rather than information arriving slowly. A third outcome against it is continuation no stronger in thin coins than in deep ones.
