In a rising, agitated market, the first up day after a run of down days is followed by further rise over the next days. The longer or deeper the down run was, measured against the coin's usual swing, the larger that rise.

The reason is my own; no source states it. In a coin that has risen for two months, most holders are in profit. Few are underwater, so few sell into a relief day to get out at breakeven. Agitation in such a market comes mostly from leverage on the long side. A run of down days is usually levered longs being cut, one stop and margin call after another.

Buyers who want the coin exist, but the wide swings make them unwilling to catch a falling price. They wait for a sign that the forced selling has stopped. The first up day is that sign. Their buying then arrives over the following days, not on the turn itself. A longer run means more forced selling cleared and more waiting buyers held back, so the recovery is larger.

This is the opposite of what is expected in falling markets, where trapped holders sell the first bounce. The difference rests on who holds the coin and who is forced to trade.

The theory is wrong if, in rising, agitated markets, the days after the first up day following a down run are no stronger than ordinary days. It is also wrong if they are weaker, or if the rise does not grow with the length or depth of the run.
