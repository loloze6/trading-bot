When a coin's day is wide against its own recent swing and the day closes near its low, the coin tends to rise over the next day. The wider the day is against its recent swing, the larger the rise. A day that ends near its low but is narrow shows little or no rebound.

The source reads such a day as exhausted selling. The mechanism below is mine. A wide day that ends at its low means selling arrived in a size well beyond what the book usually takes, and it kept coming into the close. Sellers like this are often forced rather than informed: stops firing, leveraged longs liquidated, holders capitulating. They take whatever price clears their size. The buyers who absorb that flow, such as market makers and patient dip buyers, are paid by buying below fair value. Once the forced flow is spent, nothing keeps the price down, and it drifts back up.

The day's width against the coin's own recent swing measures how far the flow exceeded normal liquidity, and so how large the concession was. A narrow day near its low is ordinary drift with no overshoot to undo. The rebound should be short, lasting the next day and not beyond, because the absorbing side closes out quickly.

The theory is wrong if wide days closing near their low are followed by no larger next-day rise than narrow days closing near their low. It is also wrong if they are followed by further falls, which would mean such days carry information rather than forced flow.
