When a coin falls much further in one day than its usual daily swing over the past month, part of that fall is given back the next day, and the larger the fall against that usual swing, the larger the next-day rise. The source states only the pattern and gives no reason; the reason that follows is mine. A day far outside the coin's normal range is seldom only new information arriving. It also contains selling that has to happen at once and cannot wait for a fair price: margin calls, stop-losses and holders who sell because the move scared them. That selling outruns the buyers who are normally present, so the price has to fall below where patient buyers would trade before enough of them step in. They are paid for taking that risk overnight. Once the forced and frightened selling is used up, the price drifts back toward where it would otherwise stand. Measuring the fall against the coin's own recent swing matters, because what counts as unusual, and so as likely forced, depends on how much the coin normally moves. A move that is routine for a wild coin would be a shock for a quiet one. The theory covers falls only. It does not claim that large rises reverse, because forced buying on rallies is rarer and less sudden. The theory is wrong if the next-day return does not grow with the size of the fall relative to the usual swing, or if the coin's largest relative falls are followed the next day by no rise or by further decline.
