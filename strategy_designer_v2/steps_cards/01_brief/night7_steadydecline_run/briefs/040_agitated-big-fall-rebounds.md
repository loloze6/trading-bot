In an agitated market, whether the coin is rising, choppy or falling, the larger a coin's one-day fall, the larger its rise over the next day. The day's own return carries the effect, not where the close sits in the day's range. The source states the pattern but gives no reason, so the reason below is mine.

When a coin swings more than usual for it, more of the selling on a down day is forced rather than chosen. Leveraged longs are liquidated, stops placed during the calmer weeks before are hit, and holders who size by recent volatility cut exposure as their risk measures jump. This selling must be done now, at any price. At the same time market makers widen their quotes and step back, so books are thin. The bigger the fall, the larger the share of it that comes from this price-insensitive flow. Once that flow ends, the price moves back toward where willing buyers and sellers would trade, and the buyers who absorbed the selling are paid for doing it. In a calm market, a fall is more often orderly selling on news or on a slow change of view. That kind of fall is not an overshoot, so it should not be given back.

The theory is wrong if, in agitated markets, the next day's return does not rise with the size of the prior day's fall, or if bigger falls are followed by further decline. The reason given is wrong if calm markets show the same relation just as strongly, because the reason ties the effect to agitation.
