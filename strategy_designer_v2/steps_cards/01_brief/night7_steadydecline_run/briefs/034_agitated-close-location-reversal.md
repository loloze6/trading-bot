In an agitated market, when a coin swings more than usual for it, where a day closes within its own high-low range points the other way for the next day. The lower the close sits in the range, the more the coin tends to rise the next day. The higher it sits, the more it tends to weaken. In a calm market the same reading carries little or nothing. The effect is graded with the close's place in the range and is mostly spent after the next day.

The source gives no reason; this one is mine. In agitated stretches more of the late-day flow is forced or hurried: stop orders, margin and leverage unwinds, and traders cutting risk as swings widen. That flow pushes the close toward the extreme of the day. It does so when books are thinner relative to order size, so the price overshoots what willing holders would accept. Once that flow is spent, liquidity providers and patient buyers or sellers who took the other side are paid as the price moves back. In calm stretches the late flow is smaller and less forced, the overshoot is small, and nothing much is left to reverse.

The theory is wrong if, in agitated markets, the close's place in the day's range has no relation to the next day's move, or if it points the same way (low closes followed by further falls). It is also wrong if the reversal is no stronger in agitated markets than in calm ones.
