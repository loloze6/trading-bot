In a calm market, of any trend, a coin that has drifted down over the past two weeks keeps falling over the following days. The deeper that decline is, measured against the coin's own usual daily swing, the larger the further fall. The claim is about the falling side only. It says nothing about whether equal rises carry on.

The source states the effect and gives one reason: in a low-swing market a steady decline is not absorbed by waiting buyers. The fuller mechanism below is mine. In a calm market a decline is made by unforced sellers. They are holders reducing exposure or taking a slow view, and they split their selling over many days instead of dumping at once. Because no single day is violent, the decline never produces the flush that dip-buyers wait for. There is no capitulation wick, no liquidation spike and no visibly cheap print. Bargain-hunting and liquidity-providing capital is therefore not drawn in, and buyers anchored to lower targets stay patient. The selling meets thin, passive demand and keeps moving price down. A larger decline relative to the coin's normal swing shows a larger seller still working through the order, so more of it remains. In agitated markets the same fall would more often come from forced flow that overshoots and is repaid, which is why the effect is placed in calm markets.

The theory is wrong if, in calm markets, deeper swing-scaled two-week declines are followed by no further fall, or by a rebound.
