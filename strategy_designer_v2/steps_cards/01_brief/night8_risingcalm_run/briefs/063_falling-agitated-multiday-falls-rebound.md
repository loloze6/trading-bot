In a falling, agitated market, a coin's fall over several days that is large against its usual swing is partly regained over the following week or two. The rebound is larger the larger the fall was, measured in the coin's own swings. In calmer or non-falling markets, the same fall tends to continue instead.

The source states this behaviour: in falling, agitated markets, falls bounce where calm-market falls continue. The reason is mine.

A falling, agitated market is where leverage is being forced out. Each leg down hits margin limits, stop orders and risk limits sized on volatility. These sellers are forced and do not care about price. Over several days their selling comes in waves into books that are already thin, because market makers widen their quotes and cut inventory when swings are large. The price is pushed below what unforced holders would accept.

Once the forced sellers are done, nobody is left to keep pressing. Absorbing capital is limited by risk and comes back slowly as swings settle. Shorts that rode the cascade take profits. Both lift the price over the following days, not in one day, because this capital returns gradually.

In a calm market the same fall comes from unforced holders leaving in pieces. It carries no overshoot to repay, which is why it continues there.

The theory is wrong if, in falling, agitated markets, large multi-day falls keep falling or show no rebound over the next weeks. It is also wrong if the rebound does not grow with the fall's size in swings, or if falls rebound just as much in falling, calm markets.
