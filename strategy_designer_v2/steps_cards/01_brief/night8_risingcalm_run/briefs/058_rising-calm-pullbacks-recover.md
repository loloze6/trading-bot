In a rising, calm market, a coin that has dipped over the past few days tends to win the dip back over the following days, and the deeper the dip against the coin's usual swing, the larger the recovery. The reason is my own; the theme states none. In a steady uptrend many traders missed the move and wait for a cheaper entry. Holders who are already in see the trend as intact and buy more on weakness. A few-day pullback in this setting is mostly profit-taking and ordinary rebalancing, selling that carries little news. Waiting buyers absorb it, and price returns toward where it was.

The calm part matters. When swings are low, holders who size their positions by recent volatility are not forced to cut. Leveraged longs are far from liquidation, so a dip does not set off a chain of forced selling. Nothing keeps pushing the price lower once the profit-taking ends. In an agitated market the same dip is more often the start of deleveraging or a turn in the trend, and buying it carries more risk. That is why the effect should be weaker or absent there.

The size link comes from the same waiting buyers. The further price falls below the recent level they anchor on, the more of them find it cheap enough to act.

The theory is wrong if, in rising calm markets, deeper few-day dips are followed by no recovery or by further falls. It is also wrong if the recovery does not grow with the depth of the dip. It is wrong as well if the effect is no stronger in rising calm markets than in rising agitated ones or in other trends.
