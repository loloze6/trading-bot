In any market, the further a coin's close falls below its highest close of the last few days, measured in the coin's own usual daily swing, the more it keeps falling over the following days. A close still at its few-day high tends to keep rising. The source states this pattern and its grading. It names no cause beyond "further selling", so the reason below is mine.

The reason I propose is that many holders judge their position against its recent peak, not against the start of the week. Traders with trailing stops, funds with drawdown limits, and holders sized to a risk budget all measure loss from the latest high. A pullback that is deep for the coin's usual swing pushes more of them past their limits. They do not all hit them at once, because their thresholds and review times differ, so their selling arrives in turn over several days rather than in one bar. Dip-buyers are not enough to absorb it, because a drop that is large against the coin's own swing reads as news rather than noise. The other end follows from the same logic. A coin at its fresh high has forced no one out, and its holders feel no drawdown to cut.

The theory is wrong if deep few-day pullbacks measured this way are followed by rebounds, or by no different drift than shallow ones. It is also wrong if the depth of the drop from the recent peak tells nothing beyond what the plain net move over the same days already tells.
