After a stretch of unusually narrow price ranges and falling volume, a coin tends to make a larger move than usual, though the direction is not known in advance. The reason is how positions build up during a quiet market. While price stays inside a tight band, traders keep placing stop orders and liquidation levels just beyond its edges. Leveraged positions also pile up, because low realised volatility makes risk models and exchanges allow larger size. Market makers quote tighter and thinner books because recent risk looks small. When price finally leaves the band, it trips this cluster of stops and forced liquidations. Their market orders hit books that were thinned during the calm, so the move runs further than the news behind it would justify. Some of the reason is my own guess. I am fairly confident about stops clustering at visible range edges and about leverage rising as volatility falls. I am less sure how much of the effect in spot prices comes from derivatives liquidations rather than from spot traders alone. The theory is wrong if quiet, compressed periods are followed by moves no larger than those after periods of normal activity. It is also wrong if what follows compression is simply ordinary volatility returning to its average, with no excess move beyond the band's edge and no surge of volume as the band breaks.
