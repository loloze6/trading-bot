Most of the source is moving-average crossover strategies. The theory they share is that price trends persist. When the average of recent prices moves from below to above the average of a longer stretch of prices, the change marks a shift in direction that tends to continue for some time rather than fade back. The same holds in the other direction for a move from above to below. The crossing is not a cause. It is a slow summary of a drift that is already under way.

The source gives no reason beyond calling this a traditional trend-following approach. The reason here is mine. Information and conviction spread slowly through a market. Some holders adjust their positions in steps, and late buyers or sellers chase a move only after it becomes visible. Many traders also watch these same averages and act when they cross, which adds flow in the direction of the move. Together these produce runs of same-direction returns that outlast the time the averages need to register them. In crypto, retail participation and attention that follows price may make this chasing stronger.

The theory is wrong if price changes after such crossings show no lasting tilt in the crossing's direction. It is also wrong if moves after a crossing tend to reverse, so that the market repeatedly crosses back and forth with no follow-through. Either would mean that what the averages pick up is noise, not persistent drift.
