Round price levels act as barriers on a single coin's chart. Price tends to stall and turn just before reaching a round number, and it tends to speed up once it trades clearly through one. The reason is how people place orders, and it is my own reasoning, not taken from a source. Discretionary traders and many simple bots put resting take-profit and entry limit orders at round figures, because those are the prices people think in. That stacks liquidity at the round level, so a move running into it meets more opposing orders and tends to fade there. Protective stops are placed differently. Traders set them just beyond the round figure so they are not hit by noise at the level itself. Once price clears the level, those stops fire as market orders in the direction of the move, and the move picks up pace for a while. Market makers who see the order clustering may trade ahead of it, which would make the turn happen slightly before the exact level. This effect should be stronger in coins and periods where retail order flow dominates, and weaker where algorithmic flow that ignores round numbers sets the book. The theory is wrong if any of these hold: bar highs and lows are no more common just short of round numbers than at nearby arbitrary levels; moves that reach a round level reverse no more often than moves reaching other levels; or price shows no extra follow-through or volume after crossing a round level, compared with crossing an arbitrary one.
