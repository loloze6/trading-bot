The day after a coin closes higher is weaker than the coin's normal drift, and the lower the coin's own trend over the past two months, the weaker that day. The grade comes from the trend, not from the up day itself. A small rise and a large one, a close near the high and a close lower in the range, a light-volume day and a heavy one should all be followed by about the same weakness at a given depth of trend. In a rising trend the effect should fade to nothing.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason; the reason here is mine. The deeper a coin has fallen over recent months, the more of its holders bought higher and sit at a loss. Many of them want out near any better price, and they treat an up day as the chance to sell. Their supply does not depend on why or how the coin rose, only on there being a rise to sell into, so it lands the next day whatever the up day looked like. A deeper decline leaves more holders underwater, so there is more of this supply. In a rising trend few holders are trapped, and up days meet buyers instead.

The theory is wrong if the day after an up day is no weaker than normal drift in falling markets. It is also wrong if that weakness does not grow as the trend deepens, or if it follows the up day's size, close or volume rather than the trend. It is wrong too if the day after a down day is just as weak, since that would make it plain downtrend drift rather than selling into rises.
