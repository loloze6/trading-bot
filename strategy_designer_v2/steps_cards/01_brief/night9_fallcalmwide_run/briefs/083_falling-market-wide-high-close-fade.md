In a falling market, calm or agitated, a day that closes near its high gives part of its gain back the next day. The wider that day's range is against the coin's own recent range, the larger the next-day fall. The source states the pattern but gives no reason; the reason here is mine.

In a falling market, buying strong enough to push a wide day to a close at its high is mostly urgent and short-lived. It comes from shorts covering, bargain hunters reacting to a sharp swing, and stops placed above recent prices. A wide range shows that this burst ran far beyond what normal two-sided trading would carry. Above it sits a stock of holders who bought higher and are waiting for strength to get out. Once the burst is spent, nothing replaces it. That waiting supply then meets thinner demand the next day, and price slips back. The wider the day, the further the burst stretched price past where the waiting sellers stand, so the more there is to give back.

In a rising market the trapped supply is small and late buyers keep arriving, so the same days should not fall.

The theory is wrong if, in falling markets, wide days closing near their high are followed by an ordinary or a rising next day. It is also wrong if the next-day fall does not grow with the day's width against the recent range, or if the same days fall just as much in rising markets.
