In any market, a day whose range is wide against the coin's usual swing and whose close sits near the day's low tends to be followed by a rise the next day. The heavier that day's volume against the coin's recent volume, the larger the rise. The rise is spent within the one day and does not build further after it.

The source states the pattern. The reason is mine, not the source's. A wide day that ends at its low on heavy volume shows a large amount of selling that had to be done at once: liquidations, stop cascades, or holders cutting risk on a deadline. Such sellers pay for urgency. Market makers and patient buyers take the other side only at a discount, and they collect that discount when the selling pressure stops, which is usually the next session. A wide low close on light volume is different. The price moved far without much trading, which points to thin books or to sellers who know something. No large forced order has been absorbed, so there is no discount owed and little to repay. Volume therefore separates a temporary, paid-for overshoot from a move that reflects lower value.

The theory is wrong if the next-day rise after wide low closes does not grow with the day's volume. It is also wrong if heavy-volume wide low closes are followed by further falls, or if their next-day rise keeps extending over the following days instead of being spent in one.
