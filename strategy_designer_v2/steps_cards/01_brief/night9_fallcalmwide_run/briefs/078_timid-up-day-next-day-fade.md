A day that closes higher by only a little, small against the coin's usual daily swing, is a sign of weakness rather than strength. The smaller the rise, the more the coin tends to fall the next day. A rise that is large against the usual swing carries no such lean.

The source reads the timid up day as exhaustion. The account of who trades comes from me. On a day when buyers try to lift the price, sellers waiting above sell into every uptick. Those sellers may be holders looking for a better exit, or traders who sell rallies. If they absorb nearly all the buying, the coin closes barely up. That shows the buying was not enough to clear the standing supply. Once that buying stops, the supply is still there and presses the price down the next day.

A large rise against the usual swing shows the opposite. Buyers overwhelmed the supply, so the fact behind the timid day is missing.

Scaling by the coin's own swing matters for this reason: a move counts as small only against what that coin normally does. I do not know why the effect would be mainly a next-day one rather than spread over several days. It may be that the leftover supply is worked off quickly.

The theory is wrong if up days with small rises against the usual swing are followed by next days no weaker than after large rises. It is also wrong if those next days are no weaker than the coin's ordinary next-day drift. It is also wrong if the weakness does not grow as the rise shrinks.
