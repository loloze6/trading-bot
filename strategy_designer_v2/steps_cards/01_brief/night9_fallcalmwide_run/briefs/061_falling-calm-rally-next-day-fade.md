In a falling, calm market, a coin's one-day rise is partly given back the next day. The larger the rise against the coin's usual daily swing of recent weeks, the larger the next-day fall. Falls in the same market are not repaid the same way. The next day is lopsided: sharp rises fade, sharp falls do not rebound.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. A coin that has slid for two months without unusual swings has a large crowd of underwater holders. Nobody forces them to sell, so they wait for a better price, and a sharp up day gives them one. That up day is often bought by thin, short-lived demand: a few shorts covering, a few hopeful dip-buyers. That demand is spent within the day, while the waiting supply keeps coming the next day. A calm decline has no forced liquidations, so a down day is orderly selling rather than an overshoot. No discount is owed to liquidity providers, and nothing pulls the price back up. I am not sure this reason is right.

The theory is wrong if, in falling calm markets, the next day after a large scaled rise falls no more than the next day after a flat day. In that case the fade is only the decline's own drift. It is also wrong if the size of the next-day fall does not grow with the size of the rise. Finally, it is wrong if large falls in this market rebound as much as large rises fade.
