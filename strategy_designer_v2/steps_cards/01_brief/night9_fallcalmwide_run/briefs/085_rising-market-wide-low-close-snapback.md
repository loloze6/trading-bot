In a rising market, calm or agitated, a day that closes near its low is followed by a rise the next day. The rise is larger the wider that day's range is against the coin's usual daily swing. The claim, the market and the grading by width come from the source. The reason is mine, because the source gives none.

A wide day that ends at its low in a rising coin is more likely a sweep than news. Stops sit below recent prices, leveraged longs get liquidated, and a few hurried sellers push through thin late-day books. All of them sell at once, whatever the price. The wider the day against the coin's normal swing, the more of the move came from this forced selling and the bigger the discount left behind.

In a rising market, many buyers already want the coin and are waiting for a cheaper price. They step in quickly once the forced flow stops, so the discount is repaid within a day rather than slowly over several days. In falling or choppy markets that waiting demand is weaker or missing, so the same day is less likely to be bought back. A narrow low close shows ordinary selling with no overshoot, so little is owed.

The theory is wrong in any of these cases:
- In rising markets, low closes on wide days do not rise the next day more than low closes on ordinary days.
- The next-day rise does not grow with the day's width.
- The coin keeps falling after such days, which would mean the wide low close marked a real turn.
