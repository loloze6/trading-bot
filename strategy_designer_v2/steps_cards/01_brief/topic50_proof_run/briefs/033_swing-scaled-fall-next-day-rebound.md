A one-day fall that is large against the coin's own recent swing is partly given back over the next day or so. The rebound grows with how unusual the fall was, and it is spent within a few days. Rises of the same unusual size are not followed by a comparable giveback.

The source reports the pattern but gives no reason. The reason below is mine. On an unusually sharp down day, part of the selling is forced or impatient: margin calls, stop-losses and holders who must get out that day. These sellers take whatever price the book offers. The buyers who absorb them have a risk budget sized to the coin's usual swing. A fall far beyond that swing exhausts their capacity, so the price is pushed past where patient money would value it. The concession is paid to those who provide liquidity, and it shrinks back once the forced flow stops and fresh buyers return the next day.

The effect is one-sided because rises seldom force anyone to buy at any price in the same way. Short sellers on spot markets are few, so a sharp rise has less forced flow behind it to overshoot and then unwind. The effect is short because it is a price for immediacy, not a change in value.

The theory is wrong if, after unusually sharp falls, the next day shows no rebound beyond the coin's usual return. It is also wrong if the rebound does not grow with the fall's size measured against the recent swing. It is wrong as well if equally unusual rises reverse just as much, since that would point to general overreaction rather than forced selling.
