In a falling, agitated market, a coin's one-day fall predicts a rise over the next day. The fall is measured against the coin's usual daily swing over the past 30 days, and the larger it is, the larger the rise. In a falling calm market the same falls do not rebound.

The source says only that capitulation days unwind most in this kind of market. The reason below is mine.

When a coin has fallen for weeks and swings more than usual, many holders are under strain. Leveraged longs face margin calls, stops sit close below the price, and holders who bought higher reach their pain limit. A large down day in that state is largely forced selling. Those sellers trade on size, not on price.

At the same time, market makers and dip buyers have pulled back, because wide swings make holding inventory risky. Each unit of forced selling therefore moves the price further than it would in a calm book. That pushes the close below what the news justifies. Once the forced flow is spent, the gap is cheap to close. Buyers and makers return, and the coin recovers part of the fall the next day.

In a falling calm market, a large fall is more likely to be steady, deliberate selling by better-informed holders. That kind of fall carries information and does not unwind.

The theory is wrong if, in a falling, agitated market:
- larger swing-scaled falls are followed by no rise or by further decline the next day; or
- the next-day rise after such falls is no larger than in a falling calm market.
