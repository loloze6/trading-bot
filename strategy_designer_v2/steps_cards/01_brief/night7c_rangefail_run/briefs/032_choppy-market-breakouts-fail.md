When a coin has gone nowhere for about two months, a daily close beyond the edge of its recent range tends to fail. Over the following days the coin comes back inside the range, and the further the close went past the edge, the larger the share of it that is given back.

The reason is my own; the operator gave none. In a trendless market most active traders are range traders. They sell near the top and buy near the bottom. Market makers set their quotes around a fair value that has held steady for weeks. A close beyond the edge in that setting is usually not new information. It is the output of stop orders and breakout entries placed just past the edge. Those orders are a limited pool. Once they have fired, nobody is left to keep pushing, and the range traders who were waiting fade the move back toward value. A larger overshoot means more stops were run and more late buyers or sellers were drawn in. That leaves a bigger imbalance to unwind, so the return should scale with the distance.

The scaling could also run the other way. A very large break may carry real news that the range traders cannot absorb, and then it holds.

The theory is wrong if closes beyond the range in trendless coins come back inside no more often than closes beyond the range in trending coins. It is also wrong if they come back no more often than chance, or if they tend to keep going. Finally, it is wrong if the size of the return does not grow with the size of the break, or shrinks as the break gets larger.
