In a choppy market, a coin that has gone nowhere for weeks and then rises in one day far beyond its usual daily swing keeps rising over the following days. The larger the rise against that swing, the larger the further rise. The effect runs one way only. An equally large one-day fall in the same market is not followed by more falling.

The source states the pattern and the asymmetry but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. In a trendless coin, the supply that caps rallies comes from range traders and holders who sell near the top of the range. A jump well beyond the usual swing pushes the coin through that supply. Those who sold or shorted near the top are now wrong, and they buy back over several days rather than at once. Spot buyers who ignored a dull, flat coin also notice the move only after it shows, and they arrive late.

A large fall in the same market finds no such forced or late flow. Range buyers waiting below absorb it, and nothing gives sellers a reason to keep pressing.

This is also why the effect would be strongest in a choppy market. In a rising market, buyers are already active and a big up day surprises less. In a falling market, underwater holders sell into strength.

The theory is wrong if, in choppy markets, large scaled up days are followed by no further rise or by a giveback. It is also wrong if the follow-through does not grow with the size of the jump, or if large down days continue just as much as large up days.
