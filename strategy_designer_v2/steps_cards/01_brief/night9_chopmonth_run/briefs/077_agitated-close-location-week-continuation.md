In an agitated market, where a coin's daily close sits within that day's high-to-low range points the way of the following week. The higher the close in the range, the more the coin rises over the next several days. Closes at the very top lead to the strongest gains, and closes at the very bottom lead to further falls, graded in order between the two.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. On an agitated day, large and often forced flows hit the book from both sides: liquidations, stop runs and squeezes. The close shows which side was still standing when that fight ended. A close at the high means buyers absorbed every wave of selling the day threw at them, so the remaining supply is spent. Shorts caught on the wrong side then cover in stages, and slower holders, who read the daily candle, follow over the next days. A close at the low means the opposite: buyers gave way, and underwater or levered holders still have selling to do. When the market is calm, the day's range is too narrow and too unforced to reveal this.

This competes with a known next-day reversal of close location in agitated markets. Both can hold if the first day pays back the late urgent flow and the week then follows the side that won.

The theory is wrong if, in agitated markets, high closes are followed over the next week by returns no better than low closes, or by worse ones. It is also wrong if the week's drift does not rise in order with the close's place in the range.
