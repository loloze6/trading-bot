In a falling, agitated market, a coin's move over the past month tends to be partly reversed over the following days to weeks. The larger the move, the larger the reversal, and this holds whether the month ended down or up. The source states the behaviour and contrasts it with falling, calm markets, where the month's move carries on instead. It gives no reason. The reason below is mine.

In a falling market with swings well above the coin's usual level, the month's path is set largely by forced flow rather than by news about value. Margin calls and liquidations of levered longs push a decline past where willing holders would price it. Holders who size their exposure by volatility cut as swings widen, which adds to the selling. A month that ended up in such a market is often a chain of short squeezes and covering, which overshoots in the same way.

Once that forced flow is spent, nothing holds the price at the extreme. The patient traders who supplied liquidity during the rush are paid as the price drifts back. A longer move stores more of this overshoot than a few days do, so the giveback should scale with the size of the month's move.

The theory is wrong if, in falling, agitated markets, month moves that are large against the coin's usual swing continue in their own direction afterwards. It is also wrong if they show no relation to what follows, or if the reversal does not grow with the size of the move.
