In a falling market, a day whose high-low range is wide against the coin's usual daily swing is followed by further decline over the next few days. The wider that day's range in units of usual swing, the larger the decline. This holds whichever way the day closes. The source states the pattern. The reason below is mine.

A wide day in a downtrend is a sign of stress, not a turning point. Many holders size their positions to recent volatility, such as risk-budgeted funds, margin lenders and leveraged traders whose collateral haircuts widen. A sudden jump in range pushes them to cut exposure. They do so over the following days, not within the same day, because risk reviews, margin calls and orderly unwinding take time. In a falling market there are few patient buyers ready to absorb this selling. Dip-buyers have been hurt repeatedly, and liquidity providers pull back after a violent session. So the delayed selling moves the price down.

The behaviour lives in a falling market only. In a rising market the same wide day draws buyers who read it as opportunity, and the forced cut is smaller.

The theory is wrong if, in falling markets, wide days are followed by no decline over the next few days, by declines no larger after wider days, or by rebounds. It is also wrong if the decline appears only after wide days that close down, which would make it continuation of the close rather than a response to the range.
