In a rising market, a sharp down day tends to be followed by a rise over the next few days. The rise is larger the more that day's fall exceeds the coin's usual daily swing. The effect lives only in a rising market and not in a choppy or falling one.

The source reports the pattern but gives no reason; the reason below is mine. After two months of climbing, many traders who missed the move are waiting for a pullback to buy, and holders sitting on gains see no reason to sell. A sudden sharp fall in that setting is often not news. It is more often forced or mechanical selling: leveraged longs liquidated, stops triggered under recent lows, or quick profit-taking. That selling pushes price below where patient buyers think it belongs. Those buyers then step in over the following days and lift the price back. A larger fall, relative to how much the coin normally swings, is a bigger overshoot and draws more of this waiting demand, so the rebound scales with it. In choppy or falling markets the waiting buyers are fewer and a fall is more likely to carry real bad news, so no such rebound is expected there.

The theory is wrong if, in a rising market, the days after a sharp down day rise no more than the days after ordinary days. It is also wrong if larger falls, measured in the coin's own swing, are not followed by larger rises. Finally, it is wrong if the same rebound appears just as strongly in choppy or falling markets, which would make it a general reversal rather than one tied to a rising market.
