In a rising market, a coin that has just risen a lot over a few days, measured against its own usual daily swing, keeps rising over the next few days. The larger that scaled rise, the more it carries on. Coins whose last few days were flat or down do not get this lift. The source claims the effect lives only in the rising market, and that outside it a large scaled rise is not followed by more gains.

The source gives no reason, so the reason below is mine. In an uptrend, buyers are many and arrive in turn. A rise that is large for the coin's usual swing stands out to traders who chase moves, to trend followers whose signals have just fired, and to holders who had sold early and now buy back. Their buying is spread over the days after the move, not done at once, so the price keeps going. Sellers are scarce in a rising market: few holders sit on losses, and short sellers who were caught must cover. In falling or choppy markets, the same rise meets holders waiting to sell at better prices, so it fades instead. Measuring the rise against the coin's own swing matters because traders notice what is unusual for that coin, not a fixed size of move.

The theory is wrong if, in rising markets, the size of a coin's recent rise scaled by its usual swing is unrelated to its move over the next few days, or if larger scaled rises are followed by smaller or negative moves. It is also wrong if the same continuation holds just as well outside rising markets.
