In a rising market, a coin rises more over the next few days when its recent swings have been quieting against its swings of the preceding months, and less, or not at all, when they have been widening. The effect is graded: the calmer the recent weeks are compared with the earlier months, the stronger the coming rise.

The source reports this pattern but gives no reason. The reasons below are mine.

First, holders who size positions by risk, such as volatility-targeting funds, leveraged traders with margin limits and market makers with inventory limits, can carry more of a coin when its swings shrink. In an uptrend, a quieting coin draws steady added buying from them over the following days.

Second, a calm advance usually means orderly accumulation, with few forced sellers left. A widening advance means late, leveraged chasing, and shakeouts and liquidations can interrupt the climb.

The effect should live only in a rising market. Outside an uptrend, quiet swings carry no buying pressure, because the added risk capacity has no trend to follow.

The theory is wrong if, in rising markets, the next few days' moves after quieting swings are no larger than after widening swings. It is also wrong if the move does not grow with how much the swings have calmed. It is also wrong if quieting swings are followed by the same excess rise outside rising markets as inside them, which would mean the trend is not where the effect lives.
