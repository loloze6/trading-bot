In a falling market, a coin's one-day move that is large against its usual swing of the past month is partly given back the next day, whichever way it went. After an outsized rise the coin lags the next day, and after an outsized fall it rebounds. The larger the day's move against that swing, the larger the move back. The source shows the pattern and gives no reason. The reason below is mine.

In a downtrend, liquidity providers and patient buyers pull back. Books are thinner and fewer traders will stand against urgent orders. Urgent flow then moves price further than its information warrants. That flow includes forced sellers cutting losses or being liquidated, and short holders rushing to cover on a bounce. Once the urgent orders are filled, nothing holds price at the extreme. The overshoot is repaid the next day to whoever took the other side, and a bigger overshoot leaves more to repay.

In rising or trendless markets, books are deeper and dip-buyers and sellers are waiting. Large moves there are more often absorbed as they happen, or carry information that persists, so the move back should be absent there.

The theory is wrong if, in falling markets, large moves against the usual swing are not followed by a next-day move the other way. It is also wrong if the move back does not grow with the size of the move, or appears after only one direction. It is also wrong if the same reversal is just as present in rising or choppy markets, since then the falling market would not be where the effect lives.
