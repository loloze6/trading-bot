Where a coin's daily close sits inside that day's high-low range tells something about the next day. The lower the close in the range, the more the coin rises the next day compared with its normal drift. The higher the close, the more it lags. The effect grades smoothly with close location rather than living only at the extremes, and it acts in every market profile.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. A close near the low means sellers were still pressing in the last part of the day. That late, impatient flow pushes the close below the price that patient liquidity providers would set without it. Some of it is forced or deadline-bound: stops, liquidations, and holders cutting before the day ends. The liquidity providers who absorb that flow want paying, and they are paid when the price drifts back up the next day once the pressure has stopped. A close near the high is the mirror case. Late chasing buyers lift the close above fair value, and the next day gives part of it back. On this view the close's place in the range measures how much one-sided urgency was left unabsorbed at the close.

The theory is wrong if the next day's drift-removed return does not fall as close location rises. That includes the case where closes near the low are followed by no better, or by worse, next-day returns than closes near the high. It is also wrong if the effect shows only through the size of the day's move, with close location adding nothing of its own.
