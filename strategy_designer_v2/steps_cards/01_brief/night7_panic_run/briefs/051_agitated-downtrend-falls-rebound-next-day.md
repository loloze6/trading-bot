In a falling, agitated market, a coin whose one-day fall is large against its own usual daily swing rises over the next day. The larger the fall in units of that swing, the larger the rise. Only falls rebound here; up days are not part of the claim.

The source states the pattern and where it lives but gives no reason. The reason that follows is mine. In a market that has been falling and swinging more than usual, a large down day is mostly forced selling: margin calls, liquidations of leveraged longs and stop cascades. These sellers trade at any price. At the same time, liquidity providers step back from books whose swings have grown, so each unit of forced flow moves price further than its information warrants. Once the forced selling is spent, the extra price concession is paid back to whoever absorbed it. Most of that happens the next day, and more after a bigger overshoot.

Elsewhere the effect should be weaker. In a rising market, waiting dip-buyers absorb a fall on the same day, so little overshoot is left by the close. In a calm falling market, falls come from orderly, unforced sellers and tend to carry on rather than revert.

The theory is wrong if, in falling, agitated markets, the day after a large swing-scaled fall is not higher than after ordinary days. It is also wrong if the next-day rise does not grow with the size of the fall, or if the rebound is no stronger there than in other market profiles.
