A coin whose price has moved far more than its usual over the past two to three months, up or down, tends to move partly back the other way over the following weeks, because much of that move was overreaction rather than lasting news.

The operator gives the overreaction. The rest of the reasoning is mine. Over a long run, buyers who extrapolate recent gains keep arriving. So do traders who chase the story, and holders who add leverage as the trend seems confirmed. This pushes price past what the news behind the move justifies. A long fall works the same way in reverse: capitulation, forced deleveraging and abandonment by discouraged holders push price below a sustainable level.

Once the move stops drawing new participants, the pressure that built it fades:
- Attention moves on to other coins.
- Late buyers sitting on gains take profit.
- Bargain hunters and patient holders step in after a collapse.

With no fresh flow to hold the extreme, price drifts back toward where steadier participants value it.

There is a strong reason it may not hold. Crypto may trend at this horizon instead. Information spreads slowly. Attention and inflows build on themselves. A coin that has run far may keep drawing capital rather than lose it.

The theory is wrong if, over the weeks after an unusually large two-to-three-month move, coins tend to keep moving the same way. It is also wrong if their later moves show no lean against the earlier move. Either way, the extreme was not overreaction that unwinds.
