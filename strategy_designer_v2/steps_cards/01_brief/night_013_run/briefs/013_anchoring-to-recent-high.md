A coin trading close to its own recent high tends to keep rising more often than a coin far below that high. When price reaches the high, it tends to break through and continue rather than turn back. The reason is mine, drawn from behavioural finance work on stocks: anchoring.

Traders treat the recent high as a reference point. As price nears it, three groups sell too early:
- holders who bought near that high and want to get out at breakeven;
- traders who see the old high as resistance;
- short-term traders who take profit because the move "looks stretched".

This selling slows the price below the level its news and demand would justify. The under-reaction stores up. When the high finally gives way, those sellers are used up, and late buyers who waited for confirmation come in. The price then catches up to where it should have been.

The same logic says a coin far below its high has no such brake. Its moves should carry less follow-through.

The theory is wrong if any of these holds:
- Closeness to the recent high carries no information about later moves of the coin's own price.
- Coins near their high do worse afterwards than coins far below it.
- Breaks above the recent high reverse back below it more often than they continue.

It is also weakened if the follow-through comes only with unusually low volume. Anchored selling should show as heavy volume just under the high, followed by easier movement once the high is cleared.
