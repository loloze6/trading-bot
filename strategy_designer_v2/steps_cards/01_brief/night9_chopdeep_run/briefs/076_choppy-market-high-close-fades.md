In a choppy market, a calm or agitated one, the nearer a coin's daily close sits to that day's high, as a share of the day's range, the more it falls over the next day. The pattern and its link to the choppy market come from the source. The reason is mine; the source gives none.

When a coin has gone nowhere for two months, most participants treat its price as range-bound. Holders and range traders keep standing offers above the market. Trend followers have no trend to join, so nobody waits to buy strength.

A close pinned at the high therefore shows urgent buying late in the day. That can be short covering, a stop being hit, or a rush of attention. It lifted price into thin offers without carrying lasting information. The sellers who met it hold inventory they took on at a discount to their view of fair value. Overnight, once the urgent flow is spent, their selling and the absent follow-up buying let the price slip back. The tighter the close is to the high, the more of the day's buying came at the end, and the larger the giveback. In a trending market, buyers who chase strength can absorb that supply, which is why the effect would be weaker or absent there.

The theory is wrong if, in choppy markets, closes near the day's high are followed by next-day returns no lower than other closes. It is also wrong if the next-day fall does not grow as the close nears the high, or if the effect is no stronger in choppy markets than in trending ones.
