In a falling market, an up day whose range is wide against the coin's own recent daily ranges is followed by a fall the next day. The wider the up day, the larger the fall. The effect is spent within that one day. It is not the start of a turn. It is a relief bounce that is given back.

The source states the pattern and reads it as a relief bounce to sell into. The reason below is mine.

In a downtrend, many traders hold shorts and stops sit above recent prices. A sudden push up can trip those stops and force covering. It can also draw bargain hunters who chase the move. That buying is urgent and carries no news. It lifts price through thin offers, and the width of the day measures how hard it pushed. Once the covering is done, nobody is left to keep buying. The sellers who supplied the move, and holders waiting to sell into strength, take price back the next day. That is how they are paid for the overshoot.

This lives in falling markets because that is where short positions and waiting supply build up. In choppy or rising markets, a wide up day more often carries real demand, so it would not fade the same way.

The theory is wrong if any of the following holds:
- In falling markets, wide up days are followed by next days no weaker than after narrow up days.
- The weakness does not grow with width.
- The fade appears just as much in rising markets.
- The weakness comes later instead of the next day.
