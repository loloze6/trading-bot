In a falling market, a coin's up day tends to be followed by a weaker next day. The fade is strongest when the downtrend is young and shallow, and it shrinks as the coin's fall so far gets deeper. In a coin that has fallen a great deal, an up day is barely given back, if at all.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is my own. Early in a decline, many holders bought near the recent highs. They sit only slightly underwater and still treat the fall as a dip. A rally brings them close to breakeven, and many sell into it to get out. Dip-buyers anchored to the recent higher prices also buy too early and are soon trapped. That overhang of nearby, willing supply meets every up day at once and pushes the next day down.

After a deep fall, the picture changes. Those recent buyers have mostly sold out or capitulated. The remaining holders are long-term or bargain buyers, who do not sell a bounce. Shorts sitting on large profits may cover into strength. So little supply waits just above the price, and a rally holds.

The theory is wrong if any of the following holds in falling markets:
- up days are not followed by weaker next days;
- the next-day fade does not shrink as the fall deepens;
- deeply fallen coins give back their up days as much as, or more than, coins only just falling.
