In a calm market, of any trend, an up day that still leaves a coin far below its recent high, measured in the coin's usual daily swings, is followed by a fall the next day. The further below the high the close sits, the larger the fall. The effect is spent within that one day.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is my own. Holders who bought near the recent peak measure their loss from it. A rise gives them a better price to cut at, so they sell into it. Their selling lands the next day, once the rise is visible at the close. The deeper the coin sits below the peak relative to its normal swing, the more such holders are under water, and the more of them use the rise to get out. This supply is unforced and arrives at once. That is why it is felt the next day and not spread over a week.

It should live in calm markets because there these sellers' orders are what move the next day. In agitated markets, liquidations, squeezes and their reversals swamp it. On a down day there is no rise to sell into, so depth should grade nothing.

The theory is wrong if, in calm markets, up days deep below the recent high fall no more the next day than up days near the high. It is also wrong if the fall appears only over later days and not the next one. It is wrong too if depth grades the next day as much after down days, or as much in agitated markets as in calm ones.
