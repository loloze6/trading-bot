In a choppy market, a coin whose recent swing runs above its own usual swing over the past year tends to fall over the following days. The fall grows with how far the swing exceeds usual. It sits mostly with the most agitated coins, and a coin swinging about as usual shows little drift.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. Many holders size their positions to the swing they see: volatility-targeting funds, risk-limited desks and levered traders whose margin and stops are set by recent moves. When a trendless coin starts swinging more than usual, these holders must cut exposure. They cut more the larger the excess, and on spot that cutting is mostly selling. In a rising market, chasers and dip-buyers would absorb this supply. In a falling one, the selling may already be spent. In a sideways market there is no trend crowd on either side to take it, so the forced trimming moves price down for some days until positions match the new swing. Agitation without a trend may also mark distribution or stress rather than news, which leaves buyers wary. I am not sure of this part.

The theory is wrong if, in choppy markets, a more agitated coin is followed by no fall, or by a rise. It is also wrong if the later fall does not grow with how far the swing exceeds its usual level, or if calm choppy coins fall just as much as agitated ones.
