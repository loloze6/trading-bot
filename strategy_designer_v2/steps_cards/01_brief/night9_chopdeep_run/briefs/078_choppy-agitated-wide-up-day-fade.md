In a choppy, agitated market, an up day whose high-to-low range is wide against the coin's recent average daily range is followed by a fall the next day. The wider the day's range, the larger that fall. The pattern and its link to width come from the source. The reason is mine, because the source gives none.

In a market with no trend but swollen swings, a wide up day is mostly forced or urgent buying. Shorts are squeezed, stops above the range are swept, and hurried buyers take whatever is offered. It rarely reflects new information about value. With no trend in place, no trend followers or chasers arrive to carry the move on. Range sellers and liquidity providers took the other side at stretched prices. Once the urgent buying is done, they unwind, and they are paid by a giveback the next day. A wider range marks more forced buying and a larger overshoot, so it brings a larger giveback.

The fade should be absent in calm choppy markets, where wide days are rarer and less often forced. It should also be absent in rising markets, where chasers absorb the overshoot and carry it on.

The theory is wrong if wide up days in choppy, agitated markets are followed by no weaker next day than narrow up days. It is also wrong if the next-day fall does not grow with the day's width. It is undercut if the same fade appears just as strongly in choppy, calm markets, since the reason ties it to agitation.
