In a falling, calm market, the better a coin's last ten days have held up, the more it rises over the following days. A coin still sliding over that span rises least, or keeps sinking.

The reason is mine; the source gives only the pattern. A calm decline is not driven by forced sellers. It is driven by holders who choose to leave, such as disappointed owners and funds cutting a losing position. They sell in pieces, without hurry, so their supply is used up gradually rather than in one flush.

While that supply is still being sold, the coin slides. When it runs out, price stops falling, and the last ten days look flat or positive. At that point, buyers who were waiting for the selling to stop see it has stopped. Because nothing in a calm market forces them to act fast, they step in slowly, and the rise builds over days rather than in one jump.

A coin whose ten days are still negative has sellers left, so it has no such rise. On this view, the effect lives mostly at the weak end, among coins still sliding. A held-up coin rises because it is no longer one of them, not because it is surging.

In an agitated market the same ten-day move would mean little. There, forced flow in both directions swamps the slow seller and the slow buyer.

The theory is wrong if, in falling calm markets, a coin's ten-day return does not grade its next days' move upward. It is also wrong if held-up coins give their gains back. And it is wrong if still-sliding coins rise as much as held-up ones.
