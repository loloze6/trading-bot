In a choppy, agitated market, where a coin has gone nowhere over two months but is swinging more than usual for it, a sizeable move over the past week tends to carry on in the same direction over the following week, more so the larger the move against the coin's usual swing.

The reason is mine, not from a source. A long, trendless stretch lets traders build positions that bet on the range: range-faders, short-option-like sellers of swings, and leveraged traders on both sides with stops and liquidation levels just outside recent prices. When agitation is high, that crowd is large and thinly margined. A week-long push in one direction catches the losing side, and those traders do not all exit at once. Their stops and margin calls sit at different distances, so their forced trades arrive in stages as the move extends. That keeps adding flow in the move's direction for days after the first push.

At the same time, the usual absorbers are weak. Liquidity providers and risk-limited funds cut their size when swings are large, so fewer of them lean against the move. Range-faders who would normally sell a rally are the ones being forced out.

This is the opposite of brief 032, which holds that such moves are information-free and are given back. The theory is wrong if, in this market, a large past-week move is followed by a next-week move that is unrelated to it or runs against it. It is also wrong if the follow-through is no stronger for larger moves than for small ones.
