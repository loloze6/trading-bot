Sharp falls that come with an extreme burst of volume, and that leave the bar's low far below its close, often mark forced selling by leveraged long holders being liquidated rather than sellers acting on new information. The theory claims these liquidation flushes overshoot and are then partly retraced, so price over the following bars recovers some of the drop instead of continuing lower.

The reason, which is my own: when price falls through levels where many leveraged longs have their liquidation points, exchanges and margin engines sell those positions at market without regard to price. Each forced sale pushes price into the next cluster of liquidation points, so the cascade feeds itself until the leveraged positions are cleared. That selling carries no view on value. Once it is exhausted, the forced sellers are gone. Patient buyers and market makers who supplied liquidity at the bottom are then paid as price drifts back toward where it traded before the cascade. The long lower wick shows that buyers already absorbed the flow inside the bar. The volume burst separates this case from a slow, quiet decline.

This differs from the general claim that heavy-volume moves persist. It holds that one kind of heavy-volume move, the forced and self-feeding kind, does the opposite.

The theory is wrong if falls with extreme volume and deep lower wicks are followed by continued decline or by no recovery, on average no different from other sharp falls of the same size. It is also wrong if the recovery appears just as often after falls without the volume burst or the wick.
