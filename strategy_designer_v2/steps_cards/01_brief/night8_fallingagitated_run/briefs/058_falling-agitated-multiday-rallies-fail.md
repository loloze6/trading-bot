In a falling, agitated market, a coin's rise over the past few days is partly given back over the following days. The larger the rise is against the coin's own usual swing, the larger the share given back.

The reason is my own; no source was given. In a decline with swings far above the coin's usual level, short sellers are crowded and heavily leveraged. A rise that lasts several days in that setting is mostly a chain of forced covering. Each squeezed short buys, lifts price into the next short's stop or liquidation, and forces more buying. That flow carries no view on value, and it ends once the leveraged shorts have been cleared.

Meanwhile, the sellers who drove the decline have not gone away. Holders sitting on losses, and funds that size positions to swings, are still above the exposure they want. A rally lets them sell more at better prices.

A larger multi-day rise means more of it came from this one-off forced buying and less from new buyers who intend to stay. When the covering is spent, this waiting supply meets thin demand and price falls back.

The effect should belong to this profile. In calm or rising markets, a few-day rise more often carries real new demand, and shorts are fewer and less levered. I do not know why the give-back would play out over days rather than at once. It may be that underwater holders sell in pieces.

The theory is wrong if, in falling, agitated markets, few-day rises are followed by further rises or by no change. It is also wrong if the share given back does not grow with the rise's size against usual swing.
