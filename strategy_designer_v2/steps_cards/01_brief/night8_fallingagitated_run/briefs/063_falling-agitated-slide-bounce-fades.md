In a falling, agitated market, a coin's first up day after a multi-day slide is partly given back the next day. The deeper the slide was against the coin's usual daily swing, the larger the giveback. What predicts the size of the fade is the depth of the fall before the bounce, not the size of the bounce.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. A deep slide in an agitated downtrend leaves many holders underwater and many shorts in profit. The first up day comes largely from shorts taking profit and from brief relief buying, and both run out within a day. That day also hands trapped holders the exit they waited for through the slide, and they sell into it. The deeper the slide, measured in the coin's own swings, the more holders are trapped and the more short profit has built up. So the bounce rests more on flow that will not come back, and more supply waits above it. This should live where the market falls and swings hard. There the slide is driven by forced and leveraged flow, and the bounce is mechanical rather than a change of view. In calmer or rising markets, real buyers can carry a bounce on. The source sees the same effect outside this profile but weaker.

The theory is wrong if, in a falling, agitated market, the next-day move after such a bounce shows no relation to the depth of the prior slide. It is also wrong if deeper slides are followed by larger next-day rises, meaning the rebound continues instead of fading.
