When a coin falls in one day by much more than its own usual daily swing of the past month, it rises over the next day by more than its normal drift. The larger the fall against that usual swing, the larger the rebound. No condition on volume or candle shape is needed.

The source reports the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. A fall that is large for this coin's recent swing is often driven by sellers who must act at once: stop orders, margin calls on leveraged spot and borrowed positions, and holders who panic. They take whatever bids are there and push the price below where patient buyers value the coin. The market makers and dip buyers who absorb that selling demand a discount for carrying the risk overnight. They are paid when the urgent selling ends and the price drifts back the next day. Measuring the fall against the coin's own recent swing matters because it separates a fall that is unusual for this coin, which is more likely forced, from one that is ordinary noise in an already wild coin.

The source also hints that the rebound is stronger when the coin has been falling for months. I do not name that as the theory's market.

The theory is wrong if days with unusually large falls, measured against the past month's swing, are followed by next-day moves no better than drift or by further falls. It is also wrong if the next-day move does not grow with the size of the fall.
