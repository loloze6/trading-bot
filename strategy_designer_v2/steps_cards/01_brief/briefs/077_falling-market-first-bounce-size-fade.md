In a falling market, an up day that comes straight after a down day is followed by a fall the next day. The larger that up day's rise is against the coin's usual daily swing, the larger the next day's fall. An up day that follows another up day is not faded this way. The effect lives in the falling trend and not in rising or choppy ones. In the source it holds across calm and agitated swings alike.

The source states the pattern but not its reason. The reason here is mine. In a falling coin, a single up day after a down day is mostly relief flow: shorts taking profit and bargain hunters stepping in after the drop. It is not new demand. Holders who are underwater, and who were just hurt again by the down day, treat that first bounce as a chance to cut. Their selling lands the next day. The stronger the bounce, the more of them it brings to their exit price, so more supply arrives.

An up day that follows another up day is different. Its buying has already met and absorbed that waiting supply, which points to demand that is not only relief. In a rising or choppy market there are fewer trapped holders, so a bounce has less supply waiting above it.

The theory is wrong if, in falling markets, the day after an up day that followed a down day does not tend to fall. It is also wrong if that fall does not grow with the bounce's swing-scaled size, or if up days that follow up days fade just as much.
