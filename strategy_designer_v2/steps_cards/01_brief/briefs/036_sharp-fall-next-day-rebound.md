When a coin falls sharply in one day, the drop is measured against its own recent daily swings. Such a fall is followed by a rebound on the next day, and the rebound is larger the more swings the fall spans. The effect is graded, not a threshold: a bigger fall in swing units brings a bigger next-day rise. It holds in every kind of market rather than one profile.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason, so the reason here is mine. A fall that is large compared with the coin's usual swing is mostly driven by urgent sellers: stop-losses firing, margin calls, holders who panic. These sellers care about getting out now, not about price. They push the price past what calmer holders think the coin is worth. Market makers and patient buyers take the other side only at a discount, and they collect that discount as the price recovers once the urgent selling is done.

The larger the fall in swing units, the more of it is forced or hurried rather than informed. So the discount, and the bounce that pays it back, grows with the size of the fall. Measuring the fall in the coin's own swings matters because a move that is ordinary for a wild coin is extreme for a calm one. Only an extreme move signals forced flow.

The theory is wrong if returns on the day after sharp falls are no higher than after ordinary days. It is also wrong if they do not rise with the fall's size in swing units, or if sharp falls tend to be followed by further falls.
