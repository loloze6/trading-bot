When a coin has been rising over the past few weeks, a day that closes in the lower half of its own range is followed by a rise the next day. A weak close is not read as the start of a turn. The rebound is larger the stronger the recent rise, and larger the nearer the close sits to the day's low. Most of it comes on the first day. Without a recent rise, a weak close carries little or no such lean.

The source gives no reason; this one is mine. In a fresh up-move, many buyers missed the earlier rise and wait below the price for a dip. They include trend followers, momentum chasers and holders adding to winners. The selling that pushes a day's close down is mostly short-term: profit-taking, small stop-outs and late intraday sellers. It is not new information, and those sellers accept a discount to get out before the close. The waiting buyers step in at the next open and repay that discount quickly. A stronger recent rise means more buyers are waiting and the crowd is more sure of the trend. A close nearer the low means a larger discount is still owed, so the rebound is larger.

The theory is wrong if any of these holds:
- After a few-week rise, weak closes are followed by next days no better than the coin's normal drift.
- The next-day rise does not grow with the strength of the rise.
- The next-day rise does not grow as the close nears the low.
- Weak closes rebound just as much when the coin has not been rising.
