In a falling, agitated market, a coin's one-day move that is large against its usual daily swing over the past month is partly undone over the following few days. The larger the day's fall in units of that swing, the larger the rebound that follows. The larger the day's rise, the larger the fall that follows. The effect scales with how unusual the day was, in both directions, and it lives only in this profile.

The source reports the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. In a falling, agitated market, leverage is high on both sides and market makers widen or pull their quotes because swings are large and the direction is against holders. A sharp fall then sets off forced long liquidations and stop-outs into a thin book. A sharp rise sets off forced short covering into the same thin book. Either way the price is pushed past where willing buyers and sellers would trade. Once the forced flow ends, the few liquidity providers still present are paid by the move back over several days, because the book refills slowly.

In calmer or non-falling markets, books are deeper and positioning is less one-sided. Large days there more often reflect information than forced flow, so they are not undone.

The theory is wrong if, in this profile, unusually large falls are not followed by rebounds larger than those after ordinary days. It is also wrong if unusually large rises are not followed by falls, if the reversal does not grow with the size of the day's move, or if the same scaled reversal appears just as strongly outside this profile.
