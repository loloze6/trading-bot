In a rising, agitated market, a day that closes near the low of its own range is followed by several days of decline. The nearer the close sits to the low, the larger the decline. A close at the low is not an overshoot that dip-buyers repay. It marks the start of an unwinding.

The source reports only the pattern. The reason here is mine. A coin that has risen for two months while swinging more than usual has drawn in many recent buyers, often levered, who bought high and hold tight stops and margin. Most of the coin's swing comes from their entries and exits. When a day ends at its low, sellers kept control into the close and no buyer stepped in to defend the price. That leaves the latest longs underwater at the day's end. Over the following days, their stops, margin calls and loss limits fire in turn, and each wave of selling pushes the next group under. Dip-buyers, who would absorb such a day in a calm uptrend, are themselves stretched and wary when the swings are large, so they wait rather than catch it. A close higher in the range shows buyers absorbed the day's selling, so it does not start the same chain.

The theory is wrong if, in rising agitated markets, closes near the day's low are followed over the next several days by a rebound or by moves no weaker than after other closes. It is also wrong if the following decline does not grow as the close nears the low.
