In a falling, agitated market, the first up day after a run of down days is given back the next day. The more of the days just before it were falls, the larger the next-day fall. What carries the signal is the day's place in the sequence, not how high it closed in its range or how large it was. A rise that breaks a string of falls fades. A rise that follows other rises fades less.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. After several falling days in a swinging downtrend, sellers who were forced out are not done; they have only paused. The first up day is driven by thin, short-lived buying: shorts taking profit, and bargain hunters stepping in early. Holders trapped by the recent falls see that first bounce as their chance to get out nearer their cost, and sellers waiting for a better price than the lows use it too. That supply meets buying that is already spent, so the next day falls. The longer the run of falls before the bounce, the more trapped holders are waiting and the thinner the buying behind it, so the larger the giveback. Once a coin has risen for several days, that waiting supply has partly been absorbed, so later rises fade less.

The theory is wrong if, in this market, an up day after falls is followed by a next day no weaker than an up day after rises. It is also wrong if the next-day fall does not grow with the number of preceding falls, or if the up day is followed by a rise.
