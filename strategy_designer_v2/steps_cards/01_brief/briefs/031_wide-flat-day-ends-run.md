After a coin has moved in one direction for several days, a day whose range is unusually wide but whose close lands near its open marks the end of that run, and over the following days the coin moves back against it. The reason is mine, not from a source. During a run, the buyers or sellers who drive it, such as trend followers, momentum chasers and late entrants, keep pressing the move. On a wide-range day that closes flat, that pressure is still there: price is pushed well into new ground. But the other side, such as profit takers, holders waiting to exit at better prices and resting limit orders, absorbs all of it and pushes price fully back. The day shows the run's fuel meeting enough opposing supply or demand to cancel it. Traders who chased at the day's extreme end up holding losing positions, and their later exits add to the move back. A quiet flat day would not carry this meaning, because a narrow range shows only a pause, not a failed push.

The theory is wrong if, after such days, the coin keeps moving in the run's direction over the next days as often as, or more often than, after other days within runs. It is also wrong if the move back is no larger than after narrow-range flat days that end runs, which would mean the wide range adds nothing. And it is wrong if the follow-through is the same whether or not a run came before the day.
