In a falling, agitated market, the nearer a coin's close sits to its highest close of the past month, the more it falls over the next few days. This holds whether the day itself closed up or down. What matters is where the price stands against the month's best close, not the last day's move.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason below is mine.

In a falling, agitated market, most holders who bought during the past month are underwater. Many hold more risk than they want, because the swings are larger than usual for the coin. The month's highest close is roughly the best exit any of them could have had. As the price climbs back toward it, more of these holders get close to breaking even. They sell to get out or to cut their size back to the larger swings. Two kinds of buying usually lift the price that far in such a market: short covering and bargain hunting. Both are short-lived and are spent by then. Nothing replaces them, so this supply pushes the price back down. The closer the price gets to that level, the more holders are near breakeven, and the larger the fall that follows. A close far below the month's high leaves those holders out of reach, so they do not sell.

The theory is wrong if, in falling, agitated markets, closes near the 30-day closing high are followed by falls no larger than closes far from it. It is also wrong if those closes are followed by continued rises, as trend-following buyers would cause. Finally, it is wrong if the effect appears only after up days, since that would make it a fading bounce rather than a matter of nearness to the high.
