When a coin closes above its recent high on the daily chart and then, within a few days, closes back inside its old range, the failed breakout tends to be followed by further decline rather than a fresh attempt higher. The reason is about who is left holding the position. A clear break above a watched high draws in breakout buyers, momentum traders and leveraged longs who treat the old high as a floor. Once price falls back below it, these late buyers are holding losses at a level they had marked as proof they were right. Many of them placed stops just under the old high or under the breakout bar. As price slips back, those stops and margin calls turn into market sells. Holders who sold near the old high before the break see their view confirmed and do not step back in to buy. Sellers who absorbed the breakout buying have shown there was real supply above. So the failure removes the buyers who would normally support a dip and adds forced sellers, and the drift down lasts for days rather than hours. This asymmetry gives profit to the short side: the failure carries more downward follow-through than an ordinary down day does. The theory is wrong if daily returns after a failed breakout are no lower than after other comparable days, or if such failures are usually followed by a second, successful break higher.
