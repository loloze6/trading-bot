In any market, whatever its trend or agitation, an up day that ends a run of down days is followed by a fall over the next few days. The fall is larger the more down days the run held, and close to nothing after a single down day. How large the up day itself was matters little.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. Each down day in a run leaves a new layer of holders under water: those who bought on the way down, and those who held through it and now want out at a better price. While the run lasts they wait for relief rather than sell into weakness. The first up day is that relief, and they sell into it and into the days after it. A longer run leaves more layers of such holders and a larger supply waiting to be released, so the fade grows with the run's length. After one down day almost no one is trapped, so there is little supply to release. Because the supply is set by the run and not by the bounce, the size of the up day should matter little. The selling is spread over days, not spent at once, because these holders are unhurried and sell in pieces as the price allows.

The theory is wrong if up days that break down runs are not followed by falls over the next days. It is also wrong if the fall does not grow with the number of down days in the run, if it is as large after a single down day as after long runs, or if it depends mainly on the up day's size rather than on the run.
