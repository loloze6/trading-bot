In a choppy, agitated market, a coin tends to do worse than usual over the following days. The weakness is concentrated in coins that are already down over the past few weeks. Coins that have drifted up within the same sideways, swinging market show little of it. The source states this behaviour but gives no reason. The reason below is mine.

A coin whose swings rise well above its usual level while it goes nowhere over two months is rarely at rest. More often it is working out a top: the earlier rise has stalled, and the holders who bought into it now face larger daily risk without the trend that justified the risk. Holders who size their position by recent swing must cut exposure as swing rises, whatever the price does. Leveraged longs face wider moves against their margin. Both are forced to sell, and nobody on the other side is forced to buy. Where the coin has already slipped over the past weeks, those holders are underwater. Each bounce becomes a chance to exit, so the selling continues rather than being absorbed. Where the coin has drifted up, holders are in profit and under less pressure, so the forced selling is smaller.

The theory is wrong if, in a choppy, agitated market, a coin's following days are no weaker than its usual days. It is also wrong if the weakness is spread evenly across coins whatever their past few weeks' move, or is stronger in coins that have drifted up.
