A coin that falls unusually hard in one day, measured against its own usual daily swing, partly rebounds on the following day. The rebound is graded: the larger the fall is in units of the coin's usual swing, the larger the next-day recovery. Ordinary down days, small for the coin, are followed by nothing in particular. The source names no market profile, so the claim covers every trend and agitation.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason below is mine. A day that is very large for the coin's normal swing is rarely made of patient, informed selling alone. Much of it is selling that cannot wait: stop orders firing in sequence, margin calls and liquidations, and holders who size positions by volatility and cut exposure when the swing jumps. These sellers pay for speed. The buyers who absorb them, such as market makers, dip buyers and arbitrage capital, step in only at a discount, so the price overshoots what the news alone would justify. Once the forced flow is spent, the discount is partly closed the next day. A bigger fall against the usual swing means more forced flow and more overshoot, which is why the rebound scales with it.

The theory is wrong if a scaled one-day fall is followed by a next-day return no higher than after an ordinary day. It is also wrong if the next-day return does not rise with the size of the scaled fall, or if the largest scaled falls are followed by further declines.
