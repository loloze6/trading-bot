In a rising, agitated market, the first down day after a run of up days marks the start of a decline that continues over the next few days. The decline is larger the longer and bigger the run that came before it, measured against the coin's usual swing.

The reason is my own, not taken from a source. A rise that comes with unusually large swings is mostly carried by late, leveraged and momentum-driven buyers. These holders entered near the top of the run. Their stops and liquidation levels sit close below the price, and they hold the coin because it keeps going up. The first down day breaks the streak they were relying on. Some sell because the momentum they followed has stalled. Others are pushed out as the drop reaches their tight stops or margin limits. Each of these sales moves price toward the next group's exits, so the selling comes in stages over several days rather than all at once.

In a calm uptrend, patient dip-buyers absorb such a day. Here the swings are already large, so those buyers wait for a deeper discount before stepping in. That leaves the late longs' exits largely unopposed.

The theory is wrong if, in rising agitated markets, the days after a first down day that follows an up run are flat or higher than usual. It is also wrong if the decline does not grow with the size of the preceding run. Finally, it is wrong if the same continuation appears just as strongly in rising, calm markets, since then agitation and fragile leverage would not be the cause.
