In any market, a coin's move over the past few weeks keeps going the same way over the following days, up or down. The move is measured against the coin's own usual daily swing over those weeks, not in raw percent. The larger the move is in those units, the further the coin carries on.

The source shows the pattern but gives no reason. The reason below is mine. A move that is large against the coin's own noise is unlikely to be random churn. It is more likely to come from a real change in what holders want to own. That change reaches the market slowly, for three reasons:
- Holders who decide on weekly or monthly reviews act late.
- Trend followers whose signals are scaled to volatility add exposure only once the move clears the coin's noise. They then buy or sell in steps.
- Funds that size positions by risk keep rebalancing for days after the move.

Each of these groups trades in the move's direction after it has been seen, so the move persists. A move of the same size in a coin that usually swings widely says little, because it sits inside the noise. Only the swing-scaled size carries the signal.

The theory is wrong if a few-week move that is large against the coin's swing is followed by no further move, or by a reversal. It is also wrong if the later move does not grow with the move's size in swing units. And it is wrong if the continuation shows only in one kind of market and is absent in the others.
