In a falling, agitated market, a one-day fall rebounds the next day. The rebound grows with the size of the fall when the fall is measured against the coin's longer-run swing. It does not grow as clearly when the fall is measured against the coin's swing over the last few weeks only.

The source states the pattern and the advice to prefer the longer yardstick. The reason is mine. In an agitated downtrend, the recent swing is itself swollen by the forced selling that marks this market. Measured against it, a truly outsized liquidation day looks ordinary, and a modest day looks small. The real overshoot, and so what the next day repays, is the move beyond what the coin's book can normally absorb. That depth is set by the coin's longer-run trading, not by the last few frantic weeks. Liquidity providers and dip-buyers size their absorbing capacity on that longer experience. So when a fall breaks far beyond it, they are stretched and demand a larger discount, which they recover the next day. A yardstick that moves with the agitation hides this grading.

The rebound itself rests on forced deleveraging overshooting thin books. Outside falling, agitated markets that selling is absent, and so is the rebound.

The theory is wrong if any of these hold:
- In falling, agitated markets, falls scaled by the recent swing grade the next-day rise as well as or better than falls scaled by the longer swing.
- Neither scaling grades the rise.
- The same graded rebound appears equally in other profiles.
