In any market, when a coin has fallen over the past several days and then closes a day near the top of that day's range, the strength is given back over the next two days. The deeper the preceding fall against the coin's usual daily swing, and the nearer the close sits to the day's high, the larger the giveback. Nothing confines this to one market profile.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. After a multi-day fall, a close pinned near the high is mostly relief flow rather than news. Shorts cover late in the day, and bargain hunters step in. Neither group carries lasting information, and both are spent once they have acted. Meanwhile holders who bought before the fall are underwater, and they treat the bounce as a chance to cut exposure. Their selling arrives over the following days and pushes price back down. A deeper fall leaves more trapped holders and more open shorts, so the relief push is larger and the supply waiting above it is heavier. A close nearer the high means that more of this late, temporary buying was pressed into the day.

The theory is wrong if any of these hold:
- High closes after multi-day falls are followed by flat or rising prices.
- The giveback does not grow with the depth of the fall, measured in the coin's own swings.
- The giveback does not grow with how high the close sits in the range.
- Low or middling closes after the same falls fade just as much, which would mean the fall alone, not the high close, carries the effect.
