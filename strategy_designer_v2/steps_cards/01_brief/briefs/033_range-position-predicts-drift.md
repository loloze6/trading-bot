Where a coin's daily close sits within its own recent high-to-low range tells which way it will drift over the following days. The higher the close sits in that range, the more the coin tends to rise afterwards, and the lower it sits, the more it tends to fall. The effect is graded through the whole range, not confined to the edges, and it is strongest for closes beyond the top of the range. This runs against the habit of fading moves toward the range edges.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason, so the reason here is mine. Many traders read a coin's place in its recent range as its trend. Systematic trend followers and breakout traders add to positions as price climbs toward and through the recent high, and cut or short as it sinks toward the low. Discretionary traders chase strength and abandon weakness in the same way. These flows arrive over days rather than at once, because traders act at different times and on different horizons. Holders sitting on losses near the bottom of the range keep selling, while shorts caught near the top are squeezed. So a close high in the range marks buying that has not yet finished, and a close low in the range marks selling that has not.

The theory is wrong if a close's position in the recent range carries no graded relation to the coin's following moves beyond its usual drift. It is also wrong if closes near the top tend to fall back while closes near the bottom tend to recover.
