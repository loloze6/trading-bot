Price and volume on spot markets behave differently in the hours just before and after the regular perpetual-futures funding settlements, which on most large derivatives venues fall at fixed times of day. When funding is strongly positive, leveraged longs who would pay it close or trim positions shortly before settlement. Basis traders who hold spot against short perpetuals can then unwind both legs. Selling spills from the perpetual into spot through arbitrage, so spot price should drift against the crowded side into settlement. After the payment is made, that pressure lifts and price should partly drift back. Negative funding should produce the mirror pattern.

The reason is my own, not from a source. It rests on a fixed and widely known payment schedule that pushes cost-sensitive leveraged traders to act at the same moment. Liquidity providers may already anticipate and price this timing, and if so the effect may be gone.

Because each coin is tested on its own bars, funding itself is not observed. The theory must therefore show in the coin's own data. Its signature would be:
- unusual volume clustering in the hours around settlement times compared with other hours of the day;
- a tendency for returns in the pre-settlement window to run against the coin's recent direction, with partial reversal afterwards.

The theory is wrong if neither shows: volume around settlement is no different from comparable hours, and returns just before and after settlement look like returns at any other time of day.
