In a calm market, of any trend, a one-day rise that is large against the coin's usual daily swing over the past month is the start of a move, not a spike. The coin keeps rising over the following several days, and the larger the rise in units of that swing, the larger the further rise.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. When a coin has been swinging less than usual, few traders are being forced to trade. Liquidations are rare, liquidity providers are not stretched, and daily moves are mostly small noise. A rise that stands far out of that noise is therefore unlikely to be forced flow that will be repaid. It is more likely the first visible trace of buyers acting on news or a change of view. Such buyers often spread their orders over days rather than paying for urgency. Other traders who size positions by recent swing also see a quiet coin as cheap in risk, so they can add after the signal without breaching their limits. Both groups arrive after the first day, so the price keeps adjusting.

In agitated markets the same rise is more often a squeeze or a liquidation sweep, which tends to fade. That is why the effect lives in the calm profile.

The theory is wrong if, in calm markets, large scaled one-day rises are followed over the next several days by no further rise or by a give-back. It is also wrong if the follow-through does not grow with the rise's size, or if it is as strong in agitated markets as in calm ones.
