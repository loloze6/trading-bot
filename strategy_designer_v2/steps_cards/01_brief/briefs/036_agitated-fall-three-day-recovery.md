In an agitated market, a coin's one-day fall that is large against its usual daily swing is followed by a rise that is not finished the next day. The rise keeps building over the following three days, and the larger the fall against the usual swing, the larger the three-day rise. The theory lives in an agitated market of any trend.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. When a coin is swinging more than usual, a large fall is likely to be driven by forced selling: margin calls, liquidations and holders cutting risk. That selling pushes the price below what informed holders think it is worth. The buyers who would absorb such a fall are themselves held back in agitated markets. Their risk limits are tight, their margin is stretched, and they are wary of catching a falling price. So the gap is not closed in one session. Capital returns over several days as volatility settles and limits reset, and the price recovers in steps. A larger fall leaves a larger gap to close and needs more of that slow capital. In a calm market a large fall is more likely to carry real news and to draw ready buyers at once, so no slow, graded recovery is expected there.

The theory is wrong if, in agitated markets, the rise after a large fall is confined to the next day and given back over the two days after it. It is also wrong if the three-day rise does not grow with the size of the fall, or if it is just as large after falls in calm markets.
