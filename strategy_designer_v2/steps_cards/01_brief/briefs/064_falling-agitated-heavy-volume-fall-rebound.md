In a falling, agitated market, a coin's down day is followed by a next-day rise, and that rise is larger the heavier the day's volume was against the coin's own recent average volume. Volume tells apart two kinds of fall that look alike on price alone.

A heavy-volume fall is a forced one: margin calls, liquidations and stop cascades push sellers who must trade now, whatever the price. They take whatever liquidity is there and pay a discount to the few buyers willing to absorb it. Once the forced flow is spent, nothing keeps price at that discount, and the absorbers' inventory is repaid within the next day.

A light-volume fall is different. Holders are drifting out, or buyers are simply absent, and no one paid a discount to get out. There is little overshoot to repay, so the next day brings little rebound.

The falling, agitated profile is where this should show most. Leverage built during the decline meets wide swings that trip forced exits, and dip-buyers demand a deep discount before stepping in. In calmer or rising markets, heavy volume is more often informed or two-sided trading, and the volume carries less of this meaning. The source states that heavy-volume falls are forced selling that is repaid. The account of who is forced and who is paid is mine.

The theory is wrong if, among down days in this profile, the next-day rise does not grow with relative volume. It is also wrong if heavy-volume falls rebound no more than light ones, or keep falling the next day as information-led moves would.
