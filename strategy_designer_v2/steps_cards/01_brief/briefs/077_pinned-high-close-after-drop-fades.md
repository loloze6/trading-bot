After a coin has fallen hard over the past few days, a day that closes at or very near its own high is followed by a further fall over the next few days. The nearer the close sits to the high, the larger that fall.

What grades the fall is the height of the close. The depth of the prior drop does not. The effect lives only in closes pinned close to the top of the range. A close merely in the upper half, or anywhere lower, carries no such fall. The source reports this pattern but gives no reason for it.

The reason here is mine and is uncertain. A drop deep for the coin leaves many holders underwater and some sellers short. A close at the very high marks a burst of urgent relief buying that drove price up into the end of the day: shorts covering and bargain hunters stepping in at once. Such a burst brings no new information and uses up the buyers who were waiting. Over the next days the holders trapped by the drop sell into the recovered price, and price slides back. A close in the middle of the range shows two-sided trade with no spent burst, so nothing is owed back. Once the drop is deep enough to have trapped holders, a deeper drop adds little.

The theory is wrong if, after deep few-day falls, closes near the high are followed by rises or by nothing different from other closes. It is also wrong if the later fall does not grow as the close nears the high, or if it is carried by the depth of the drop instead.
