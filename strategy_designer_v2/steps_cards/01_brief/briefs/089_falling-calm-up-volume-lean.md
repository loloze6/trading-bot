In a falling, calm market, a coin rises more over the following days when its trading volume over recent weeks has leaned toward its up days and away from its down days. The lean shows a buyer absorbing supply before the price shows it.

The reason is mine; no source is given. A calm decline is orderly. Holders who are not forced to sell reduce exposure in pieces, and few people are trying to buy. A new buyer who is not forced either, accumulating a position over weeks, cannot hide all of its volume. It tends to show on the days when its buying lifts the price. Meanwhile the down days grow lighter as the remaining sellers thin out.

So the split of volume between up and down days measures who is now doing the trading, while the price still reflects the old trend. Once the sellers' supply is spent, the buyer's continued demand moves the price up. The market adjusts to this slowly, because the trend still looks down to anyone reading price alone.

The effect should live in calm falling markets only. In an agitated decline, volume is dominated by liquidations, squeezes and hedging that land on whichever day the swing falls. That would make the up/down split noise rather than a footprint of accumulation.

The theory is wrong if a coin's later returns in a falling, calm market do not rise with the share of recent volume traded on up days. It is also wrong if a lean toward up-day volume is followed by flat or weaker days. It is wrong as well if the same relation is just as strong in agitated or rising markets, since then calm accumulation would not be the reason.
