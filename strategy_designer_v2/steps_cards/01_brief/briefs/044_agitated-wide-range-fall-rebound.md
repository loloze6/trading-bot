In an agitated market, a coin's one-day fall is followed by a rise the next day. That rise grows with two things together: how large the fall was against the coin's usual daily swing, and how wide the day's high-to-low range was against its usual range. The two compound. A large fall on a wide day rebounds most. A large fall on a narrow day rebounds little or not at all. The rebound is spent within that next day.

The source reports the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine.

A wide range on a falling day in an already agitated market marks selling that had to cross the book. Margin calls, liquidations and stop orders take whatever liquidity is there, so price swings far through thin quotes. The buyers who absorb that flow are stretched in inventory and risk. They demand a discount, and the wider and deeper the push, the larger the discount. Once the forced flow stops, the discount is repaid as price returns toward where unforced trading would have left it. A narrow-range fall looks more like orderly repricing on information, which there is no reason to give back. In calm markets, forced selling is rarer and books are deeper, so a fall says less about forced flow.

The theory is wrong if, among agitated falls of equal size, wide-range days rebound no more than narrow-range days. It is also wrong if the next-day rise does not grow with the size of the fall, if the effect is as strong in calm markets, or if the rebound keeps building over the following days instead of being spent in one.
