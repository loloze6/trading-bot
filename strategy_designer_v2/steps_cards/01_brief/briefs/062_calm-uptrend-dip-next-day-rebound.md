In a rising, calm market, a coin's one-day fall that is large against its recent daily swing is partly regained the next day. The larger the fall in swing units, the larger the next-day rise. The source states the pattern and its link to the calm profile but gives no reason. The reason here is mine.

When a coin has been rising and its swings have been small, a large one-day drop is unlikely to be news. More often it is one hurried seller, a sweep of stops, or a burst of liquidations meeting a book that was thinned by the quiet. Several things then go unbroken. The rise has not been broken. Holders who size their exposure by volatility see no reason to cut, because the coin's swing is still low. Patient buyers who have been waiting for a lower price in a market they trust take the discount. The overshoot is repaid quickly, within a day, to whoever absorbed it.

In an agitated rising market, the same fall is less unusual. It is more likely to carry real deleveraging or information that keeps working. So the rebound there should be weaker or less reliable.

The theory is wrong if any of the following holds:
- In rising, calm markets, large swing-scaled one-day falls are not followed by an above-usual next-day rise.
- The rise does not grow with the size of the fall.
- The rebound is no stronger in calm than in agitated rising markets, which would make calm irrelevant.
