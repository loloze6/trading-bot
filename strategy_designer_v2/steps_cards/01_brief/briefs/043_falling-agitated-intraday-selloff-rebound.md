In a falling, agitated market, the size of a coin's drop from the day's open to its close predicts its next day against itself. The bigger the drop within the day, the bigger the rise the following day. The rise is spent in about one day and does not build over the days after. The claim is about the day's own open-to-close move in raw size, the selling done inside the session. It is not about where the close sits in the day's range or how big the fall is against the coin's usual swing.

The reason is mine, not the source's. In a coin that has fallen for weeks and swings more than usual, a day of steady selling is mostly sellers who need to get out now: holders cutting losses, or leveraged longs closed by their risk limits. The buyers who take the other side in a nervous, thinly bid book are short-term liquidity providers. They are already wary after weeks of losses, so they take on inventory only at a discount, and the discount grows with the amount they have to absorb that day. When the selling stops, the price is no longer held down, and the discount is paid back as a quick rebound. Because the discount is the price of one day's absorbed flow, not new information, it should close fast, mostly within the next day.

The theory is wrong if, in this profile, a larger open-to-close drop is followed by no next-day rise or by a further fall. It is also wrong if the rebound keeps growing over several days, since a one-day inventory discount cannot explain that. It is also in doubt if the same relation is as strong outside this profile.
