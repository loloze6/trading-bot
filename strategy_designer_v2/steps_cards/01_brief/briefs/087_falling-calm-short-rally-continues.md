In a falling, calm market, a coin that has risen over the last few days keeps rising over the following days. The larger that rise is against the coin's usual daily swing, the further it carries. The coin is in an orderly decline: it has been falling for weeks while swinging less than usual.

The source states only the pattern. The reason below is mine, and I hold it with little certainty.

An orderly decline has no forced flow. Nobody is being liquidated, and shorts are not crowded enough to be squeezed. So a rise that is sharp for the coin cannot easily be explained as a short-covering burst that will fade. It more likely comes from willing buyers who have decided to accumulate. Such buyers, acting in a quiet market, split their orders over days. The first days' rise is then the visible start of a larger order still being filled. A stronger start marks a larger order still to come.

At the same time, the sellers in a calm decline are patient and unforced. When the price lifts, they step back and wait rather than sell into it. That leaves the buying to push the price further.

In an agitated decline, the same rise would more often be a squeeze. Squeezes overshoot and reverse. This is why the continuation would live in the calm falling market only.

The theory is wrong if, in falling calm markets, larger swing-scaled few-day rises are followed by no higher, or by lower, returns over the next days than small rises. It is also wrong if such rises are reliably given back.
