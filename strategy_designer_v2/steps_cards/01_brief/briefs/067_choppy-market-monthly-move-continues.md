In a choppy market, a coin's plain return over the past month points the way of its move over the following week. After a strong month it keeps rising, and after a weak month it keeps falling. The bigger the month's move, the bigger the move that follows. This holds after rises and after falls alike. It is the opposite of what range trading in a trendless market would expect.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason, so the reason here is mine, and it is uncertain. Some slow capital acts on a coin's last month: allocators who rebalance toward recent winners, and trend followers whose signals look back about a month. They add to their positions in pieces over the following days. In a rising or falling market, this flow is mixed into the larger flow of the longer trend and of the holders positioned in it. It then shows no clean order of its own. In a choppy market there is no longer trend, so this month-driven buying or selling is the main directional pressure left. Because the two-month window is flat, a large monthly move there often means the coin has just turned, and the newly repositioned crowd has not finished acting.

The theory is wrong if, in a choppy market, the following week's move is unrelated to the past month's direction or goes against it. It is also wrong if larger monthly moves are not followed by larger moves the same way, or if the effect appears only after rises or only after falls.
