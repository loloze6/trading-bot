In a calm market, a one-day rise that is large against the coin's usual daily swing is followed by more rise. The follow-through does not come the next day. It comes the day after, and it is larger the larger the rise was against that usual swing. The effect lives in calm markets, where a big up day stands out against quiet days, so it is more likely to reflect a real change in demand than noise.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine, and it is uncertain. The next day may be flat because short-term liquidity providers and holders who were waiting for a rally sell into the jump. That selling offsets fresh buying for a day. Meanwhile slower participants, who act on daily closes after they confirm, plus attention-led buyers who notice the outlier move late, arrive a day later. Once the short-term supply is spent, their buying pushes the price up. In an agitated market a large day is common and draws no such late attention, which would explain why the effect stays in calm markets.

The theory is wrong if, in calm markets, the second day's return after a rise does not grow with the size of that rise against the usual swing. It is also wrong if the follow-through shows just as much on the first day as on the second. And it is wrong if large calm-market rises are given back over the following two days.
