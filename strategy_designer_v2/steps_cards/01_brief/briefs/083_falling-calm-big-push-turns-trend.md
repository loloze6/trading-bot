In a falling, calm market, a coin whose close sits far above its recent average keeps rising over the following days. The distance is measured in the coin's own recent daily swing. The effect is concentrated in the largest pushes: a modest lift above the average carries little, and only a push that is large for the coin's swing carries on.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. In a calm decline, the selling is orderly and unforced. Holders drift out, and nobody is squeezed. That is why a large push above the average in this market is unlikely to be short covering or a liquidation snap-back: calm markets hold little of that fuel. It more likely marks new demand that has outweighed the steady supply, and that flow is not spent in one go. The traders who must follow are slow:
- Trend followers short or flat on the decline are pushed out of their position as the price clears its average.
- Allocators who went underweight during the decline buy back in steps once the decline looks broken.

So a large push in a calm downtrend is the early part of a turn, not a bounce to be sold. Small pushes stay inside the decline's ordinary noise, where underwater holders still sell into them.

This is not the claim that stretches carry both ways. It says only large upward stretches carry, as a sign of a turn.

The theory is wrong if, in falling, calm markets, closes far above the average are followed by no rise or by a giveback over the next days. It is also wrong if the following rise is no larger after the biggest pushes than after modest ones.
