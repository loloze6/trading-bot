In a choppy, calm market, a coin whose close has moved away from its recent average price keeps moving the same way over the following week or two. The farther it stands from that average, measured against the coin's own recent daily swings, the larger the move. This holds on both sides: above the average it rises, below it falls. A sideways market would usually be expected to pull price back toward its middle, so the claim is that it does the opposite.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason; the reason here is mine. In a calm, sideways market nobody is forced to trade. No liquidations, no margin calls, no panic. So when price drifts away from its average, the drift is unlikely to be an overshoot that liquidity providers will be repaid for. It more likely reflects holders who have changed their view and are building or cutting positions in pieces, without hurry. Range traders who anchor on the average lean against the drift, and that slows the repricing further, spreading it over many days. Because swings are small, a distance that is large in swing units stands out as a deliberate shift rather than noise. That would explain why the effect grows with the distance and with the horizon.

The theory is wrong if, in choppy calm markets, a coin's swing-scaled distance from its average is followed by no move, or by a move back toward the average. It is also wrong if the grading holds on only one side of the average.
