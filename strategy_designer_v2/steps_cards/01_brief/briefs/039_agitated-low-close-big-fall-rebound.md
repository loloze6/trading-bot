In an agitated market, a down day that both closes near its low and falls far beyond the coin's usual recent daily swing is followed by a next-day rise. The rise grows with the two together. A low close alone, or a large fall alone, carries less of it. The source states this pattern: the two features combined predict the next day better than either alone, in agitated bars and not in calm ones. It gives no reason. The reason below is mine.

A large fall shows that much selling hit the book. A close at the low shows the selling was still going on at the end of the day, with no buyer stepping in to lift the price before the close. Together they point to selling that had to be done rather than chosen: stop orders, margin calls, and holders cut out of leveraged positions. That kind of seller trades at any price and pushes the close below where patient buyers would set it. The next day, the forced flow is spent and those buyers return, so the price recovers part of the overshoot.

The effect would live in agitated markets because forced selling is common when swings are large and leverage is being cut. In a calm market, a fall closing at its low is more often an orderly move with no overshoot to give back.

The theory is wrong if, in agitated markets, such days are followed by next-day returns no higher than either of these:
- large falls that close mid-range;
- small falls that close near the low.

It is also wrong if the effect is as strong in calm markets.
