In a falling, agitated market, a coin's up day that is large against its usual daily swing of the past month tends to be given back over the next one to three days. The larger the rise is in those swing units, the larger the giveback.

The pattern comes from the source. The reason is mine; the source gives none. A sharp up day in a coin that has been falling for weeks while swinging more than usual is unlikely to be news. More likely it is forced buying. Leveraged shorts built up during the decline are squeezed through books that the agitation has thinned, so price is pushed past where willing buyers stand. Once that forced covering is done, the buying stops. Two groups then meet the bounce as sellers: holders trapped higher up in the decline, who use the relief to get out, and liquidity providers who sold into the squeeze and now unwind. The overshoot is repaid. The agitation matters because it marks the market in which leverage, forced flow and thin books are most present. In a calm decline, a rise is more often orderly buying with no overshoot to repay.

The theory is wrong if, in falling agitated markets, large scaled up days are followed over the next one to three days by returns no lower than usual. It is also wrong if the following weakness does not grow with the size of the rise.
