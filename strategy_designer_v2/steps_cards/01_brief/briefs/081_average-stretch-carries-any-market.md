In any market, rising, choppy or falling, a coin whose daily close stands far above or far below its recent average, measured in its own usual daily swing, keeps moving the same way over the following week or two. The further the close stands from the average, on either side, the larger that later move. The effect is not a feature of trendless markets alone. It is a general carry of the stretch, which a range-only reading hides.

The source gives no reason; the reason here is mine. A close far from its recent average, measured against the coin's usual swing, means the coin has moved far relative to its own noise, so the move is less likely to be random. Many participants are anchored to that average. Holders who bought near it, range traders who fade departures from it, and allocators who size by recent swing all reprice slowly. They first resist the move, then give up in stages, and each stage pushes the price further the same way. Trend followers keyed to price leaving its average add their orders late. Because this adjustment spreads over days, the move continues instead of reversing.

The theory is wrong if closes stretched far from their recent average are followed, over the next week or two, by moves no larger than usual, or by moves back toward the average. It is also wrong if the graded carry appears in only one trend profile and is absent or reversed in the others.
