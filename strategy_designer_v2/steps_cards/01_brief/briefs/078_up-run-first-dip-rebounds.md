When a coin has closed up several days in a row, the first day that closes down is not the start of a reversal. It is followed by a rise the next day. The further the run of up days carried the price, measured in the coin's usual daily moves, the larger that rise.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. A strong run of up days draws buyers who act slowly: trend followers who wait for confirmation, holders who add over days, and late buyers who notice the move only after it is large. The first down day after the run is mostly short-horizon traders taking profit and range sellers fading the move. That selling is not new information, and it does not change the demand that built the run. The slower buyers who have not yet bought treat the lower price as a chance to enter, so the dip is absorbed and the price lifts the next day. A longer, stronger run has drawn more attention, so more of this demand is still waiting, and the rebound is larger.

The theory is wrong if the next-day move after a first down day does not grow with the run's size in usual moves. It is also wrong if, after the strongest runs, the first down day leads to further falls rather than a rise, which would mean the down day marks spent demand rather than a pause.
