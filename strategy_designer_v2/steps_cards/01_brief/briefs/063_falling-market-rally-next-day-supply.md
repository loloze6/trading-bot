In a falling market, calm or agitated, a coin's one-day rise that is large against its usual daily swing is followed by a fall the next day. The larger the rise, the larger the fall. The give-back is quick: it is spent by the next close and does not run on over the following days. The source states the pattern and that it is short-lived, but gives no reason.

The reason here is mine. After two months of decline, many holders are underwater and waiting for any relief to get out. A sudden strong up day is the price they have been waiting for. Because they are already decided sellers rather than slow deliberators, they sell into it at once. Their supply lands within the next session and pushes the price back down. Once that waiting supply has been sold, nothing more presses on the price, so the fall stops after a day. In a rising market few holders are trapped, so the same up day is not met by this supply.

This is a one-sided claim about rises. It says nothing about equal falls. I am not certain of the reason. Short covering that runs out would also fit the next-day fall. It fits the quick end of the fade less well.

The theory is wrong if any of these hold in falling markets:
- large scaled up days are not followed by next-day weakness;
- the weakness does not grow with the size of the rise;
- the weakness keeps building over several days instead of ending after one;
- the same next-day fall appears just as strongly after large up days in rising or choppy markets.
