In an agitated market, a coin's one-day fall that is large against its usual daily swing is followed by a rise the next day. The rise is larger when the fall is larger and when the day's volume was heavy against the coin's recent average, and the two compound. A large fall on light volume brings little or no rebound.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason, so the reason here is mine. Heavy volume on a sharp down day marks selling that had to happen that day: margin calls, liquidations, stop orders and holders cutting risk sized to recent swings. Those sellers do not care about price. They pay a discount to whoever absorbs their flow. In an agitated market, the buyers who absorb that flow already carry stretched inventory and tight risk limits, so the discount must be wider before they step in. Once the forced selling is done, the price returns toward where patient buyers value the coin, and the absorbers are repaid. A fall on light volume is more likely a quiet repricing by holders who are not forced. It carries no discount to repay. In a calm market, books are deep and risk capacity is free, so forced flow is absorbed near fair value and leaves little to recover.

The theory is wrong if, in agitated markets, heavy-volume large falls are not followed by a stronger next-day rise than light-volume falls of the same size. It is also wrong if the rebound does not grow with the size of the fall, or if heavy-volume falls keep falling the next day.
