In a falling market, calm or agitated, a coin that closes up on a day when it is still down over the last few days tends to fall the next day. The wider that up day's high-to-low range is against the coin's usual daily swing, the larger the next-day fall. The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason below is mine.

Inside a decline that is still running, an up day comes from short-lived buying: shorts taking profit, bargain hunters, and sellers pausing. It does not come from a change in who holds the coin. A wide range on such a day shows a hard fight. Buyers lifted the price a long way, and sellers met them through the day. Holders who are underwater and waiting for relief to exit are the supply. A wide bounce brings price up to where many of them sold or bought, and it shows them the exit. Once the covering and bargain buying are spent, nothing is left to absorb that supply, and price falls back. A narrow up day draws little of this supply, so it gives little back. In a rising market, holders are not waiting to exit and dip-buyers keep supporting price, so the effect should not appear there.

The theory is wrong if, in falling markets, such up days are followed by next-day rises or flat days. It is also wrong if the next-day fall does not grow with the day's range against usual swing, or if the same fade appears just as strongly in rising markets.
