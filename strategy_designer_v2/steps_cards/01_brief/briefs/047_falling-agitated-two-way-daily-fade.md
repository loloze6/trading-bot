In a falling, agitated market, a coin's one-day move is partly reversed the next day, in both directions and in proportion to its size. The larger the day's fall, the larger the next day's rise. The larger the day's rise, the larger the next day's fall. The reversal is spent within that one day and does not carry into the days after.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason below is mine. In a market that has been falling for weeks and swinging more than usual, much of each day's move comes from forced trading that carries no news.

- On down days, stretched longs are liquidated or stopped out. Their urgent selling pushes the price below where patient buyers will take it, and the discount is repaid the next day.
- On up days, shorts who piled into the decline are squeezed and cover in a rush. Holders trapped higher up then sell into the relief, so the squeeze gives way the next day.

In both cases liquidity providers absorb a one-sided burst and are paid for it once the burst ends. The effect is short because the forced flow comes in single bursts. It belongs to the falling, agitated market because only there are both crowded longs under strain and crowded shorts present at once. In a rising market, short squeezes are rare and the burst on up days is missing.

The theory is wrong if, in falling, agitated markets, large one-day falls are not followed by next-day rises, or large one-day rises are not followed by next-day falls. It is also wrong if the next-day reversal does not grow with the size of the day's move, or if it continues beyond the next day.
