After a down day, a coin tends to rise the next day. The rise is larger the nearer the down day's close sits to its low, measured as a share of the day's high-low range. A down day that closed at its low is followed by the strongest next-day rise. A down day that recovered most of the way to its high is followed by little or none. The source gives only the pattern, not why it happens. The reason that follows is mine.

A fall within a day is partly an overshoot by sellers who had to sell: stopped-out holders, margin calls, hurried exits. Liquidity providers and patient buyers absorb that selling and are paid when the price recovers. The close's place in the range shows how much of the recovery is still owed when the day ends. If the close sits at the low, the selling ran until the end of the day. Nobody had yet stepped in, so the whole recovery is still to come, and it comes the next day when fresh buyers and market makers return. If a down day closes near its high, the absorbers already bought the low inside that same day. The recovery happened before the close, and nothing is left to pay out the next day.

This differs from a general close-location reversal: the claim is only about down days, and only about how much of a fall's rebound remains unpaid.

The theory is wrong if, among down days, those closing near the low rise no more the next day than those closing near the high. It is also wrong if closes at the low are followed by further falls.
