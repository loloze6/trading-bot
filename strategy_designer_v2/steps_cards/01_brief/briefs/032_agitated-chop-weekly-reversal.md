When a coin's market is choppy and agitated, with large daily swings in both directions and no lasting trend, its net move over the past week tends to be partly given back over the following week. The larger that weekly move is compared with the coin's usual swings at the time, the more of it reverses.

The operator's reason is that without a trend, a large swing carries no lasting information. It is noise, so it has nothing to sustain it. I add a reason for why the noise reverses rather than just stopping. In agitated conditions much of the volume comes from traders who must or want to trade at once. These include leveraged holders cut by margin calls, stop orders firing on both sides, and short-term traders reacting to the last swing. Their flow pushes price past where patient buyers and sellers value the coin. Market makers and patient traders take the other side only at a discount, and they are paid when price drifts back as the rushed flow dies down. Agitation raises both the share of such flow and the discount demanded. That is why the giving back should be strongest when the market is most agitated and trendless.

The theory is wrong if, in choppy agitated periods, a coin's past-week move is followed by a next-week move in the same direction, or by moves unrelated to it. It is also wrong if the giving back is no larger than in calm or trending periods, or if it does not grow with the size of the weekly move.
