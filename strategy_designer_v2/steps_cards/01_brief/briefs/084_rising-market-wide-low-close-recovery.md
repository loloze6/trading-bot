In a rising market, calm or agitated, a day that closes near the bottom of its range is followed by a rise over the next three days. The rise is larger the wider that day's range is compared with the coin's own ranges over recent weeks. The trigger is where the close sits, not whether the day closed down. What matters is that the selling was still pressing at the close, and that the day was unusually wide.

The source gives no reason, so this one is mine. In an uptrend, a wide day that ends at its low is usually a sweep. Stops and leveraged longs placed under recent prices are triggered in a cascade late in the session, and the close is set at a discount before buyers can respond. The buyers who matter in a rising market are trend holders and allocators who add on weakness. They are slower than intraday liquidity providers. They decide on the close they have seen and buy over the following sessions, so the repayment spreads over days rather than coming in one bounce. A wider day means more forced flow and a deeper discount, so there is more to repay. In falling or choppy markets these waiting buyers are fewer, so the effect should be weaker there.

The theory is wrong if, in rising markets, wide days closing near their low do not rise over the next few days. It is also wrong if the rise does not grow with the day's width, or if it is no larger in rising markets than in other markets.
