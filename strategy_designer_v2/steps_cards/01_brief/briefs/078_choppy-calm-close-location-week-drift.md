In a choppy, calm market, where a coin closes in its day's range points to its move over the following week. The nearer the close sits to the day's high, the more the coin rises over the next several days. The nearer it sits to the day's low, the more it falls. The effect is graded by how near the extreme the close is. It does not show the next day, and may even reverse slightly then; it builds over the week.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason, so the reason here is mine. In a trendless, quiet market the crowd is mostly range traders who sell strength and buy weakness, and no trend followers are there to chase. A close pinned at one end of the day shows that the day's last and most persistent flow beat those faders. In a calm market that flow is unforced, so it is more likely to carry information or a lasting change in holdings than a liquidation. The faders resist at first, which gives the small next-day giveback. They then give way and cover over the following days, while slow holders reprice. So the move spreads over a week instead of landing at once.

The theory is wrong if, in choppy calm markets, closes near the day's high are not followed by stronger weeks than closes near its low. It is also wrong if the week's move does not grow with nearness to the extreme, or if the relation reverses. It is also wrong if the effect is no stronger in choppy calm markets than in falling or choppy agitated ones.
