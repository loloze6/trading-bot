In a falling, agitated market, a coin's one-day move, measured against its recent daily swing, is partly undone the next day in either direction. A sharp up-day gives back and a sharp down-day bounces, and the bigger the day's move against the swing, the bigger the reversal. The effect is spent after that one day.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. In a coin that has fallen for weeks and swings more than usual, the books are thin and positioning is crowded on both sides. Longs bought on the way down and shorts added late. A sharp fall is pushed by forced sellers: liquidated longs and holders stopped out. A sharp rise is pushed by forced buyers: shorts covering in a squeeze. Neither group trades on value, so each move overshoots what the thin book can absorb. Liquidity providers and patient traders take the other side and are paid when the price settles back the next day.

The reason ties the effect to this profile. In a calm decline, forced flow is rare and the book absorbs it. In a rising agitated market, shorts are fewer, so squeezes and liquidation cascades are less one-sided.

The theory is wrong if, in falling agitated markets, the next day's return does not weaken as the day's scaled move grows. It is also wrong if only falls reverse while rises continue or show nothing, if the reversal is as strong in calm or rising markets, or if it lasts well beyond the next day.
