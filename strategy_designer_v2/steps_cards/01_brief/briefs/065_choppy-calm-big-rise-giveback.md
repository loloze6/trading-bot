In a choppy, calm market, a coin's one-day rise that is large against its usual daily swing is given back over the following few days. The larger the rise in units of that swing, the larger the giveback. Ordinary up days carry no such reversal.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason, so the reason here is mine. In a quiet, trendless coin, the people who hold it are mostly range traders and patient holders anchored to the range they have lived in for weeks. A sudden large up day in such a market is more likely a burst of urgent buying, such as a short squeeze, a single large order or a brief wave of attention, than a lasting change in value. It pushes price through a calm, thin book.

There are no trend followers to carry it on, because the coin has no trend. Range sellers and holders who have been waiting to exit near the top of the range sell into the jump. The liquidity providers who were lifted unload their inventory as the urgency fades. Price drifts back toward where the range has kept it, and the further the push went beyond the usual swing, the more there is to repay.

This should belong to the trendless quiet market. In a rising market, chasers and trend followers would instead take a large up day as confirmation and extend it.

The theory is wrong if, in choppy calm markets, large scaled one-day rises are followed by flat or further rising prices over the next days. It is also wrong if any giveback does not grow with the size of the rise.
