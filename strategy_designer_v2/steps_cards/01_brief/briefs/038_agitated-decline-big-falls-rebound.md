In a falling, agitated market, the larger a coin's one-day fall, the more it rises over the next day.

This differs from a calm decline. In a calm decline, selling comes from holders who sell steadily into each pause, so weakness tends to continue. Once the decline turns agitated, the reversal shows up.

The source only observes the pattern and the contrast with the calm case. It gives no reason. The reason below is mine.

A decline that is swinging more than usual is one where leveraged longs and margin positions are under strain. A large down day there is likely to be a forced sweep. Liquidations and stop orders sell at any price into books that market makers have thinned because risk is high. The price is pushed below what willing holders and buyers would pay. Once the forced flow is spent, the gap is closed by:
- buyers who were waiting;
- shorts taking profit.

The bigger the fall, the more of it is likely forced, and so the bigger the rebound. In a calm decline, the same size of fall is more likely to be orderly, discretionary selling that carries information. That selling does not overshoot.

The theory is wrong if any of these hold in a falling, agitated market:
- the next day's return does not grow with the size of the day's fall;
- large one-day falls are followed by further declines;
- the rebound after large falls is no stronger than after falls of the same size in a falling, calm market.
