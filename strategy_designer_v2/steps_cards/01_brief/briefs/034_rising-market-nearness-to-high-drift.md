In a rising market, how close a coin's daily close sits to its recent high, measured in units of the coin's own usual daily swing, sets how much it rises over the following days. The rise grows steadily as the close nears the high and grows further the more the close stands above it. A coin that has slipped many swings below its high in a rising market drifts flat or lower.

The source reports the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. In a rising market, new money hunts for the coins that are visibly winning. A close at or above the recent high is the plainest public sign of that. It draws trend followers, breakout buyers and people who missed the move and wanted proof first. It also pushes short sellers whose stops sit above the high to buy back. These buyers arrive over days, not at once, so the price keeps drifting up.

A coin far below its high in the same rising market tells a different story. Its rally has failed. Holders who bought near the top sell into any recovery to get out flat, and the chasing money goes elsewhere. The behaviour should live in rising markets because only there is chasing money plentiful enough to keep coming. In a choppy market, breaks tend to fade instead.

The theory is wrong if, in rising markets, the rise over the following days does not increase with nearness to or distance above the recent high. It is also wrong if coins far below their high rise as much as coins at it.
