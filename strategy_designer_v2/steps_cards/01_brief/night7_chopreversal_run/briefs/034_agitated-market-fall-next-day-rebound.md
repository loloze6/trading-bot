In an agitated market, with any trend, a coin's one-day fall that is large against its usual daily swing is partly regained the next day, and the larger the fall, the larger the regain. In a calm market the same fall carries no such rebound. Rises do not show the mirror effect.

The pattern comes from the source. The source gives no reason, so the reason below is mine. When a coin swings more than usual, leverage is stretched and margins are tight. A sharp fall then sets off liquidations, margin calls and stop orders. These sellers must sell now, whatever the price. Market makers and patient buyers take the other side only at a discount, and they hand it back the next day once the forced selling stops. The bigger the fall, the more of it is forced, so the bigger the discount and the bigger the regain.

In calm conditions a fall of the same size is less likely to be forced. It more often reflects news, so the price stays where it went. Rises are not symmetric because forced buying of spot, such as short squeezes, is rarer and smaller than forced selling of longs.

The theory is wrong if, in agitated markets, the next day's return does not grow with the size of the prior day's fall. It is also wrong if calm markets show the same graded rebound, or if large one-day rises in agitated markets reverse just as falls do.
