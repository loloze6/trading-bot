In a falling, agitated market, a coin's one-day fall that is large against its usual daily swing is partly reversed the next day. The larger the fall, the larger the rebound. The effect lives in this profile, not in agitated markets generally: in a rising, agitated market a large one-day fall is not followed by a graded rebound, and in calm markets of any trend it is not either.

The source reports the pattern but gives no reason. The reason below is mine.

After two months of decline with heavy swings, many holders sit on losses. Leveraged longs are close to their margin limits, and lenders and exchanges are quick to cut positions. In such a market a sharp down day is mostly forced or panicked selling: liquidations, margin calls, stop-losses and capitulation by holders who can no longer bear the loss. None of these sellers trade because of new information about value. They sell at any price for one day, and buyers who absorb that flow demand a discount. Once the forced flow is spent, the price recovers part of that discount the next day. A bigger fall means more forced flow and a bigger discount, so a bigger recovery. In a rising market, holders sit on gains and leverage is less stretched. A sharp fall there is more often deliberate selling, such as profit taking or a reaction to news, and it carries no comparable overshoot.

The theory is wrong if, in falling, agitated markets, large one-day falls are followed by no rebound, or by a rebound that does not grow with the fall. It is also wrong if the same graded rebound shows equally in rising, agitated markets.
