In an agitated market, a coin's net move over one day is partly reversed the next day, in both directions and in proportion to its size. A larger fall is followed by a larger rise, and a larger rise by a larger fall. The day's net change alone carries this. Where the close sits in the day's range adds nothing once the net change is known. The reversal is spent within the next day.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. When a coin swings more than usual, the traders who absorb order flow face more risk per unit held. These are market makers and patient counter-traders, and they have tighter limits. One-sided flow from stop-outs, margin calls, chasing and hedging therefore moves price further than the information behind it would justify. It moves price in either direction. The absorbing side takes the other side only at a discount, and is paid back as price drifts toward value once the urgent flow stops. The size of the day's net move measures how much was pushed through, so it measures how much comes back. In a calm market, providers carry flow cheaply and the overshoot is smaller.

The theory is wrong if any of these holds in agitated markets:
- the next day's move is unrelated to the day's net move, or has the same sign;
- only falls reverse and rises do not, or the reverse;
- the reversal is no stronger than in calm markets;
- the reversal keeps building over the following days instead of ending after one.
