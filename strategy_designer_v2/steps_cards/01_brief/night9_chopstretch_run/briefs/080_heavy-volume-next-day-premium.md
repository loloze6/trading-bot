A coin's trading volume on a day, measured against its own recent average volume, says something about the next day's return whatever way the day itself moved. After a day much busier than usual the coin tends to rise the next day. After a day much quieter than usual it tends to lag. The effect is graded: the heavier the day against the coin's usual volume, the better the next day; the lighter, the worse.

The source states only the pattern and gives no reason. The reason here is mine. Heavy volume is a sign that a coin has drawn attention. On spot exchanges most of the newly attracted traders can only buy, because shorting a spot coin is costly or impossible for them. So the attention turns into net buying that arrives over the following day, after the busy day that drew it. A quiet day is the reverse: attention has drained away and fresh buyers are absent. Holders who sell then meet only thin bids, and price drifts lower.

The theory is shown wrong in either of these cases:
- Heavy-volume days that closed up are not followed by a rise, so that the premium comes only from heavy days that fell and then rebound, an overshoot being repaid rather than attention-driven buying.
- Unusually quiet days are followed by no weaker next day than ordinary days are.
