In any market, rising, choppy or falling, a coin whose daily swings over the last few weeks have shrunk against its swings over the last few months tends to rise over the following days. The deeper the contraction, the larger the rise.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. Many holders size their positions by recent swing: volatility-targeting funds, risk-parity books, and leveraged traders whose margin and position limits loosen as realised moves fall. When a coin's swings contract, these holders can carry more of it at the same risk, so they add exposure. They do it gradually, as their measures update, which gives a steady buying flow over the next days. The flow grows with the size of the contraction. Sellers have no matching forced reason to act when swings quiet, so the flow is one-sided.

This differs from the rising-market version already indexed in where it lives. The claim is that the buying comes from risk sizing alone, whatever the trend, not from trend followers adding on.

The theory is wrong if any of these shows:
- coins whose short swings have contracted against their longer swings rise no more over the next days than other coins;
- the rise does not grow with the depth of the contraction;
- the link appears only in rising markets and vanishes or reverses in choppy and falling ones, since that would point to trend-driven buying rather than risk-sized buying.
