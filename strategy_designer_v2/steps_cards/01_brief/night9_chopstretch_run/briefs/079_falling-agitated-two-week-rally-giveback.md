In a falling, agitated market, a coin that has risen over the past two weeks gives part of that rise back over the following week. The larger the rise, measured against the coin's own recent daily swing, the larger the giveback. The source states this pattern but gives no reason for it. The reason below is mine.

A coin that has been falling for two months has a layer of holders who bought higher and are now underwater. When price climbs back for two weeks, it moves toward the prices where many of them entered. In a calm decline they might wait. In an agitated one, the swings have already hurt them and their risk limits, so they use the recovery to cut exposure near break-even.

A rise that is large against the coin's usual swing reaches deeper into that overhead supply, so more sellers are waiting there. It also draws more short covering and bargain buying, which is spent once the move stops. With the buying used up and the supply still there, price drifts back down.

The theory is wrong if, in falling, agitated markets, larger swing-scaled two-week rises are followed over the next week by the same or further rise, or if the size of the rise tells nothing about the week that follows.
