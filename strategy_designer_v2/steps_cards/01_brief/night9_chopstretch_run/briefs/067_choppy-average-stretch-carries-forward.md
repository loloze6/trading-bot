In a choppy market, one that has gone roughly nowhere over the past two months, calm or agitated, a coin whose close stands far from its recent average keeps moving the same way over the following days. "Far" is measured against the coin's own recent daily swing. A close well above the average is followed by more rise, and one well below it by more fall. The larger the stretch is for the current swing, the larger the move that follows, and this holds on both sides of the average.

The source reports the pattern but gives no reason for it. The reason here is mine. In a market without a trend, most holders and market makers anchor to the range. They treat any move away from the middle as noise and lean against it, selling stretches up and buying stretches down. That leaning slows the price but does not stop it. When the stretch is large against what the coin usually does, it is more likely to carry real news or a lasting shift in demand than random drift. The range-anchored crowd sees this only after the price has stayed away from the middle for a while, then gives up its fade and reprices in steps. The move unfolds over days rather than at once.

The theory is wrong if, in choppy markets, large stretches from the recent average are followed by a return toward it. It is also wrong if what follows bears no relation to the stretch's size, or if the carry shows on one side of the average only.
