After a stretch of unusually narrow price ranges, a coin tends to make a large move, and the first decisive four-hour bar out of the narrow range points the way it continues over the next one to three days.

The reason is mine. A quiet market is one where buyers and sellers agree on value and where many traders, seeing little movement, place their stop orders close to the edges of the narrow range. When news or a large order pushes price through an edge, those stops turn into market orders in the same direction, and traders who waited for the range to resolve join the move. Four-hour bars show such a quiet stretch and its end earlier than daily bars, while carrying less noise than hourly bars.

Because the move after a quiet stretch lasts days, it is large next to the cost of a trade.

The theory is wrong if moves after narrow-range stretches are not larger than moves after ordinary stretches, or if the direction of the first decisive bar out of the range does not predict the direction of the following days.
