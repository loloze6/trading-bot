A coin's distance from its recent high carries information about its coming drift in every kind of market, not only a rising one. Measure the distance in units of the coin's own recent daily swing. The closer a daily close sits to the recent high, and the further it stands above it, the more the coin tends to rise over the following days. The further it sits below the high, the weaker its drift.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason, so the reason here is mine and uncertain. Measured against the coin's normal swing, a close at or beyond the recent high is a move larger than the coin's usual noise would produce. That suggests real demand, such as accumulation by informed or patient buyers. Many participants only act once a move has persisted: holders who sold early and buy back, discretionary traders waiting for confirmation, and systematic flows that react with a lag. This slow uptake of the information spreads the price adjustment over days instead of completing it at once. The same slow adjustment would let weakness far below the high keep drifting lower. I do not know why this would hold equally against a falling or choppy backdrop, where overhead supply might be expected to absorb such demand.

The theory is wrong if, across trends, the distance from the recent high scaled by the swing shows no relation to later returns or a reversed one. It is also wrong if the relation lives only in rising markets and vanishes in falling and choppy ones.
