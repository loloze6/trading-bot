A one-day move that is large compared with the coin's own recent daily swing partly reverses over the next day. This holds for rises as well as falls and in every kind of market. The larger the move in swing units, the larger the give-back.

The source states only the pattern. The reason is mine. A day with an unusually large move is usually a day of one-sided, hurried order flow. Traders chase a jump or flee a drop, stops fire, and leveraged positions are forced open or shut. Someone has to take the other side of that flow. In thin crypto books those are market makers and patient traders. They step in only at prices that pay them for holding inventory they did not want. So the close of a hurried day sits past the price that slower, unhurried trading would set. Once the urgent flow stops, those liquidity takers unwind their inventory and the price drifts part of the way back.

Measuring the move against the coin's own swing matters for this reason. What counts as hurried for a quiet coin is ordinary for a wild one. So the overshoot should grow with the move's size relative to the coin's usual day, not with its raw size.

The theory is wrong if large swing-scaled one-day moves are followed the next day by no move back, or by continuation. It is also wrong if the give-back does not grow with the move's size. Finally, it is wrong if large rises do not reverse while large falls do, because then only forced selling is at work, not paid liquidity in both directions.
