In a choppy market, a coin that closes beyond the high or low of its recent range tends to fail. Over the following days it comes back inside the range, and the further the close went past the edge, the larger the share of the break it gives back.

The reason is mine, not taken from a source. A coin with no clear trend over two months has no strong flow of new information pushing it one way. Its range is held by traders who sell near the top and buy near the bottom, and by holders who have learned that the edges do not last.

Stops and breakout orders cluster just beyond the edges. A push through an edge fires them. That buys or sells into a thin book without any new view of value, so the price overshoots. The further the close goes past the edge, the more of the move came from these forced and chasing orders rather than from news. More of it is therefore unsupported. Range traders and value holders fade the overshoot once the stops are spent, and the price drifts back.

In a rising or falling market, the same break would more often have real flow behind it, so the effect should live in the choppy market only.

The theory is wrong if, in a choppy market, daily closes beyond the recent range keep going or stay outside as often as they return. It is also wrong if larger breaks give back no more of their move than small ones, or if the return inside is no stronger in choppy markets than in trending ones.
