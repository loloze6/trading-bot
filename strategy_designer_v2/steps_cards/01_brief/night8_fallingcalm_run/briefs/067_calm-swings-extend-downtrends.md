In a falling market, the calmer a coin's daily swings have been over the past month compared with its own past year, the further it keeps falling over the following days. The more agitated those swings, the weaker that further fall, and it may even turn into a rise. The effect is a gradient: it should grow steadily as the coin's recent swing sinks below its usual level, not switch on at one threshold.

The source reports the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine.

A quiet decline is an orderly one. Holders sell in pieces because they choose to, not because they are forced out. Nobody is liquidated, no one sells in panic, and no overshoot builds up that liquidity providers must later be paid back. So nothing pulls price back up, and the holders who have not yet sold remain a supply still to come.

Quiet swings also draw no bargain hunters. Those buyers wait for a visible flush before they step in, so a calm fall meets few of them.

An agitated decline is different. It has usually already forced out its weakest holders and overshot. Squeezes and rebounds follow, and these offset the trend.

The theory is wrong if, in falling markets, coins in calm stretches fall no further over the following days than coins in agitated stretches. It is also wrong if the further fall does not grow as the swing becomes calmer, or if calm falls tend to rebound.
