In a choppy, calm market, a coin that closes at the very bottom of its day's range tends to fall again the next day. The selling that pinned the close does not reverse; it carries on.

The source states this pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. In an agitated market, a close at the low is often the mark of forced, hurried selling. Stretched liquidity providers absorb it at a discount and are repaid the next day, which is why low closes there tend to rebound.

A calm, sideways market lacks that forced flow. No liquidations are cascading and no one is cutting risk because swings have grown, so a close at the low is less likely to be an overshoot. It is more likely the trace of an unhurried seller still working a larger order, such as a holder giving up on a range that has gone nowhere. That seller keeps selling into the next session. Liquidity providers in a calm book are not stretched and have no discount to recover, so nothing pulls the price back. The low close is information about unfinished supply rather than a temporary imbalance.

The theory is wrong if, in choppy calm markets, closes at the bottom of the range are followed by next-day rises, or by moves no different from those after other closes. It is also wrong if the same next-day weakness after low closes appears just as strongly in agitated markets, where the theory says forced flow should produce rebounds instead.
