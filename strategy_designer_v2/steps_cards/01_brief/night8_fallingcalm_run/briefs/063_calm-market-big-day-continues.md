In a calm market, a coin's one-day move that is large for its usual daily swing carries on in the same direction over the following several days. The larger the day's rise against that swing, the more the coin keeps rising, and the larger the day's fall, the more it keeps falling.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. When a coin has been swinging less than usual, few traders are forced to act: there are few liquidations, few stop cascades and little hurried hedging. A day that stands out against that quiet background is therefore more likely to carry new information or a deliberate repositioning than forced flow. Such a move invites no quick repayment to liquidity providers. Instead it is absorbed slowly. Attention-limited holders notice it late. Traders who size positions by volatility and those who follow trends act on it only after it shows. Large holders who started the move spread their orders over days. The price therefore keeps drifting the same way. In an agitated market, by contrast, large days are more often forced and partly reverse, which is why the effect should live mainly where swings are calm.

The theory is wrong if, in calm markets, large swing-scaled one-day moves show no continuation over the following days. It is also wrong if they reverse, if continuation does not grow with the move's size, or if it is no stronger in calm markets than in agitated ones.
