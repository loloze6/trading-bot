In a falling market, a coin tends to rise on Sunday, the day after Saturday's close. The rise is larger the more the coin fell over the week before. After a flat week there is little or no Sunday rise.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason below is mine and is uncertain.

In a falling market, much of the selling during the week comes from sellers who work weekday hours or who are forced out: desks cutting risk, funds meeting redemptions, and levered holders being stopped or liquidated. A deep weekly fall means a lot of this selling was pushed through the books. The price at Saturday's close can still carry the discount those sellers paid. On Sunday most of these sellers are absent.

The buyers who remain are different. Some are individuals buying a cheap coin on their free day. Some are short-sellers who took profit on the week's fall and cover before the new trading week brings back liquidity and hedging flow. Weekend books are thin, so this one-sided buying moves price more than usual, and the week's overshoot is partly repaid. The effect lives in falling markets because only there is a large backlog of forced weekday selling to be repaid. In other markets, a weak week is more often ordinary two-way trading.

The theory is wrong if, in falling markets, the day after Saturday's close does not rise more than other days. It is also wrong if that rise does not grow with the prior week's fall, or if it is just as large outside falling markets.
