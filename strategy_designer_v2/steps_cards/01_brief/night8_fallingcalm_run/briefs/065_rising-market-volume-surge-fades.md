In a rising market, the more a coin's trading volume over the past month has grown against the month before, the weaker its next several days. A large surge in volume is followed by a smaller rise, or by a fall.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. In a rising coin, a month of swelling volume marks the trend's crowded phase. Late buyers arrive in numbers, drawn by the rise itself, often with leverage. Early holders use that demand to sell into strength. Once the burst of new buying has passed, the marginal buyer is already in. The positions left behind are recent, levered and quick to exit. Supply from distributing holders keeps coming, so price stalls or gives back. In a rising coin whose volume has not grown, the trend is carried by holders who sit. No crowd has yet been drawn in, and none is waiting to leave.

This explains the surge side: a crowd arriving and then leaving. It is a different reason from the dry-up side, where sellers are said to be exhausted. It lives in rising markets because only there does a volume surge mean buyers chasing. In falling markets the same surge means forced selling still running.

The theory is wrong if, in rising markets, coins whose monthly volume grew most go on to rise as much as, or more than, those whose volume held steady or shrank. It is also wrong if the weakness after volume growth does not grow with the size of the growth.
