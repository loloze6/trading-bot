When a coin falls steadily over several weeks without panic, with daily ranges and volume staying ordinary, the decline tends to continue over the next days. The larger the fall over those weeks, the stronger the further fall.

The operator gives the main reason: holders sell into every pause. A calm decline never forces a flush. No liquidation cascade or capitulation day clears out the holders who want to leave. Their selling stays spread out, and each stall or small rise becomes a chance to get out. So the overhang of willing sellers does not shrink the way it does after a crash.

I add two reasons of my own. First, holders who are underwater often wait for a smaller loss before selling, and the deeper the fall, the more of them there are, so supply sits just above the price. Second, in a quiet market bad news may spread slowly, and buyers who would step in after a dramatic drop see no clear bargain. Both are offered as plausible, not established.

The calm condition matters. In a violent fall, forced selling can exhaust itself and leave room for a rebound. In a calm fall, that release never comes.

The theory is wrong if, in calm declines, a larger fall over past weeks is not followed by a larger further fall over the next days. It is also wrong if calm declines are followed by flat or rising prices, or if calm declines continue no more than declines that came with panic.
