In a rising, agitated market, a coin that has run far above its recent average gives back part of that run over the next days, and the further above the average it stands, the larger the giveback. The reason is my own reading of who trades at that point. In a rising market whose swings are larger than usual for the coin, the last stretch of a run is seldom carried by new holders who mean to stay. It is carried by levered chasers, by short sellers forced to cover, and by momentum buyers who arrive late. All of them are fragile. Their buying is spent once the squeeze ends. Their leverage, sized for wide swings, makes them sell on the first pullback. Holders with profit targets sell into the gap above the average, and the liquidity providers who sold to the chasers get repaid as the price settles back.

In a calm rising market the same distance above the average is more likely built by steady, information-led buying, which can keep going. That is why the effect should live in the agitated profile and be weak or reversed in the calm one.

The theory is wrong if, in rising agitated markets, a larger distance above the recent average is followed by an equal or further rise over the next days. It is also wrong if the following move shows no relation to that distance. Its claim about the market is wrong if the giveback is just as strong in rising calm markets.
