In a falling, calm market, a coin whose close stands far below its 20-day average, measured in its own recent daily swings, keeps falling over the following days. The further below, the more it falls. A stretch below the average is not an overshoot waiting to be repaid. It is a decline still being worked through.

The source states the pattern and gives no reason. The reason here is mine. In a calm decline nobody is forced out. There are no liquidation cascades and no margin calls. So a price far below its recent average was not pushed there by hurried flow that liquidity providers absorbed at a discount, and there is no discount to win back. The distance instead reflects unforced holders who have decided to leave but sell in pieces, over days, to avoid moving a quiet market. Buyers of dips see no capitulation to step in front of and hold back. The larger the gap, the larger the backlog of patient selling still to come. In an agitated decline, forced selling does overshoot and gets bought back, which would mask or reverse the effect. That is why the theory places it in calm markets only.

The theory is wrong if, in falling calm markets, coins far below their 20-day average in swing units fall no more over the following days than coins near it. It is also wrong if they rise, rebounding toward the average as a stretched market would.
