A coin that closes up on the day, but still near the bottom of its high-low range of the past few weeks, tends to give that rise back the next day. The lower the close sits in that range, the larger the giveback.

The pattern comes from the source. The reason is mine, because the source gives none. A coin near the bottom of its recent range has a crowd of recent buyers above the price who are now under water. Many of them want out at a smaller loss. A one-day rise is the first chance they get, so they sell into it the following day. The rise itself is often driven by things that carry little news: short covering, bargain hunting, or market makers rebuilding stock. Once that buying is spent, nothing is left to hold the price up against the waiting supply.

The deeper the close sits in the range, the more of the range's holders are trapped above it, and the larger that supply. A rise that has already lifted the coin into the upper part of its range has run through most of those sellers. That is why the effect should fade as the close sits higher, and could even turn into continuation.

The theory is wrong if up days that close low in the recent range are followed by next-day moves no weaker than those after other up days. It is also wrong if the next-day move does not get weaker as the close sits lower in the range, or if the grading runs the other way.
