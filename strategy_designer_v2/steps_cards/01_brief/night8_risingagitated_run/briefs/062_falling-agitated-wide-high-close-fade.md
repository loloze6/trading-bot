In a falling, agitated market, a coin that closes near the top of a day whose range is wide against its recent daily swing gives part of that close back the next day. The higher the close sits in the range, and the wider the day, the larger the give-back. The two compound: a high close on a wide day says more than either alone. The effect is spent within about a day.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. In a falling, agitated market, a wide day that ends near its high is usually forced buying, not new information. Shorts are squeezed, short liquidations are triggered, and bargain hunters chase the bounce late in the session. The width of the day measures how much of this urgent flow hit the book. The close near the high shows that the flow was still pressing when the day ended. The liquidity providers who sold to it end the day holding stretched short inventory. Once the forced buyers are done, nothing new arrives to hold the price up. Underwater holders in the downtrend sell into the higher level, and the providers unwind, so the price slips back. Because this is paying back an overshoot rather than responding to news, it should stop once that payment is made.

The theory is wrong if any of these holds in falling, agitated markets:
- high closes on wide days are followed by next-day rises or by no change;
- narrow days closing high fade as much as wide ones;
- the decline keeps building over several days, which would point to information rather than an overshoot being repaid.
