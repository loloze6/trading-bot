In a falling, agitated market, a coin's sharp one-day rise is followed by a fall over the next few days. The rise is measured against the coin's own usual daily swing, and the larger it is, the more of it is given back. This is about rises only. The claim does not say that equal falls in the same market behave the same way.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. When a coin has been falling and swinging hard, a large up day is rarely new information arriving. It is mostly forced buying in thin books: shorts stopped out or liquidated, and quick bargain hunters chasing the bounce. That buying comes once and is soon spent. The selling it meets is slower and keeps coming. Holders who bought higher and are deep under water use the bounce to cut their losses. Holders who size their positions by volatility, and who are already overexposed in a swinging market, sell into strength over the next days, not within the hour. The bigger the squeeze, the further price is pushed above where that patient supply is willing to sell, so the more there is to give back. In a calm or rising market the same rise has fewer trapped sellers waiting and more willing buyers, so the effect should be weak or absent there.

The theory is wrong if, in falling, agitated markets, larger swing-scaled one-day rises are followed by flat or stronger returns over the next few days. It is also wrong if the giveback does not grow with the size of the rise.
