In a falling, agitated market, an up day is partly given back on the very next day, and the larger the rise is against the coin's own recent daily swing, the larger the next day's fall. The source states this pattern and that it lives in the agitated half of the falling market, not the calm half. It gives no reason; the reason that follows is mine.

In a coin that has been falling for weeks while swinging more than usual, sharp up days are seldom driven by new buyers with a view. They come mostly from forced buying: shorts covering in a hurry as their losses or margin calls bite, with books thinned by the agitation. Liquidity providers who sell into that urgent buying are left short at a stretched price. Once the covering is done, nobody keeps paying that price. Holders still underwater from the decline sell into the strength, and the providers buy back lower. A bigger rise, scaled to the coin's usual swing, means more forced covering past the price the market would accept, so more is given back. In a calm falling market, rises are mostly unforced trades by holders adjusting slowly, so there is little overshoot to repay. That is why the effect should live in the agitated profile only.

The theory is wrong if, in falling, agitated markets, larger swing-scaled up days are not followed by larger next-day falls. It is also wrong if the same graded giveback appears just as strongly in calm falling markets, where no forced covering is claimed.
