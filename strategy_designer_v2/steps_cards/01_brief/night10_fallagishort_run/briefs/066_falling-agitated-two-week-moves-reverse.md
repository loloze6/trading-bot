In a falling, agitated market, a coin's two-week move that is large against its own recent daily swing tends to be partly reversed over the following days, in either direction. After a strong two-week fall it recovers part of it. After a strong two-week rise it gives part of it back. The larger the move in swing units, the larger the reversal. Distance below the two-week high behaves the same way: the deeper the coin sits below it, the more it recovers.

The source states the direction: in this profile both measures point the opposite way to the continuation seen in choppy markets. The reason is mine. In a falling, agitated market, large multi-day moves come mostly from forced flow rather than new information. On the way down, leveraged longs are liquidated and holders who size by risk cut as swings widen. On the way up, crowded shorts are squeezed. Both push price past where unforced holders value it. The forced flow is spent once positions are closed. Buyers who were absorbing it, or sellers waiting for a better price, then pull price back.

This lives in falling, agitated markets because there leverage and swing-based risk limits bind hardest. In calmer or sideways markets, moves are more often deliberate repositioning that keeps going.

The theory is wrong if, in falling, agitated markets, large swing-scaled two-week moves are followed by more movement the same way, or by nothing. It is also wrong if only falls reverse while rises do not.
