In a falling, agitated market, a coin that closes in the upper part of its day's range after a losing week first gives that bounce back over the next couple of days. Then, over the following days, it recovers more than it gave back. The fade is short-lived, and what follows it is a rebound, not a resumed decline.

The two-phase path comes from the source. The reason is mine. The first phase is the familiar relief-bounce supply. Holders trapped by the week's fall, and levered longs still under margin pressure, use the strong close to sell. Short covering that lifted the close is finished. So the next days give the bounce back.

The second phase is why this lives in a falling, agitated market. There, the selling into the bounce is often the last forced flow of a deleveraging wave. Once it is spent, few sellers are left. Liquidity providers who absorbed the forced flow at a discount are repaid, and buyers waiting for the flush step in. In calmer or non-falling markets this capitulation step is missing, so the fade is not followed by a rebound.

The theory is wrong in either of two cases:
- In a falling, agitated market, such a close does not fall over the next days.
- The fall is not followed within days by a rise that exceeds it, so the coin is no higher than at the bounce close.
