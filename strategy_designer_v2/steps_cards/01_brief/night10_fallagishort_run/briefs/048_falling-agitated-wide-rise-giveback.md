In a falling, agitated market, a day on which the coin closes higher is partly given back the next day. The giveback grows with two things together: how large the rise is against the coin's usual daily swing, and how wide the day's high-to-low range is against its usual range. A large rise on an ordinary day, or a wide day with a small rise, carries little of the effect. The largest rises on the widest days are followed by the largest falls. This is the mirror of the wide-range fall rebound, applied to up days in a falling market.

The source reads such a day as a short-lived squeeze rather than a turn. The explanation of why is mine. In a falling market, many traders are short or hold hedges against further decline. When agitation is high, a sharp push up forces them to buy back at any price, through stops, liquidations or risk limits. A wide range shows that this buying was urgent and swept through thin books. A large rise shows how far it pushed. Together they measure how much of the day's gain was paid by forced buyers rather than by new holders. Once the covering is done, no fresh demand holds the price. Sellers who still expect the decline sell into the higher level. The premium is repaid within a day.

The theory is wrong in either of these cases:
- In this profile, large, wide up days are followed by no next-day fall, or by a rise.
- The next-day fall does not grow with the range on top of the size of the rise.
