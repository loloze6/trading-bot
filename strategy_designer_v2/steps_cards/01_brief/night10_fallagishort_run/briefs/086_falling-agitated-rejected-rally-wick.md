In a falling, agitated market, a day that trades well above its open and then gives that rise back, leaving a long upper shadow against the coin's usual swing, is followed by further falls over the next few days. The longer the shadow, the larger the fall.

The reason is my own; no source gives it. In a deep, swinging decline many holders are underwater. They have left sell orders resting not far above the price, waiting for any relief to get out. When short covering or bargain buying lifts the price during the day, it runs into that waiting supply. The rise is sold back down before the close. The long upper shadow is the visible mark of this: buyers tried, and sellers at the level above had more to sell. That supply is not used up in one session. Holders who did not get filled, and those who now see the bounce fail, keep selling over the following days. Buyers who chased the intraday rise are left trapped and add to the selling.

This differs from a plain low close. A low close alone in this market tends to mark forced selling that is repaid. Here the information is the rejected rise, not where the day ended.

The theory is wrong if days with long upper shadows in falling, agitated markets rebound or drift like ordinary days over the following days. It is also wrong if longer shadows bring no larger later falls, or if the effect is no stronger in this market than in calm or rising ones.
