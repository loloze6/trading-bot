In an agitated market, a coin's ordinary up day is partly given back the next day. An ordinary up day is a rise that stays within the coin's usual daily swing, not an outsized jump. The larger the rise within that usual swing, the larger the give-back. In a calm market such rises are not reversed.

The source states the pattern and its link to agitation but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. When a coin is swinging more than usual, much of its daily movement comes from flow that carries no news. Leveraged traders cover shorts or are stopped out. Momentum traders chase intraday moves. Risk limits force positions to be resized. Market makers with less depth and tighter inventory limits than usual absorb this flow. They let price move further than the information warrants and recover by leaning against it the next day.

In calm markets, flow is smaller and books are deeper. Day-to-day moves are then more likely to reflect slowly absorbed information, which does not revert. Under this reason, the up day is a temporary push paid for by the impatient side. Its size within the usual swing measures how much non-informational pressure there was to undo.

The theory is wrong if any of the following holds in agitated markets:
- Ordinary rises are followed by next-day returns no lower than after other days.
- The next-day give-back does not grow with the size of the rise.
- Calm markets show the same reversal as agitated ones, which would mean agitation is not where the effect lives.
