A coin's move over the last week keeps going in the same direction over the next few days, whether the week was up or down. The push grows with how unusual the week's move was against the coin's own recent daily swing. What counts is the move's size in the coin's own swings, not in percent: a week that is large for a quiet coin carries on as much as one that is large for a wild coin.

The reason is mine; the source gives none. Many trend followers and volatility-sized systems measure a move against recent swing, so a week that stands out against it draws their orders over the following days. Discretionary traders who notice the move late add to it. Protective stops and leveraged positions on the losing side are hit in turn as the move extends. None of this flow arrives at once, so the price adjusts in steps rather than in one jump.

The behaviour is expected to fail in a falling, agitated market. There, forced selling and squeezes push prices past where holders value them, in both directions, and the next days repay that overshoot instead of extending it. The behaviour is expected everywhere else.

The theory is wrong if the next few days' moves do not rise with the size of the week's move in swing units. It is also wrong if they rise only after up weeks and not after down weeks. Finally, it is wrong if large scaled weeks outside falling, agitated markets are followed by reversal as often as by continuation.
