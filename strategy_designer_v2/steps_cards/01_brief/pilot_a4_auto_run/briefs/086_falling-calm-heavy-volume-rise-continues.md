In a falling, calm market, an up day made on volume heavy against the coin's own recent volume is followed by further rise over the next days, more the heavier the volume. Light-volume up days are not followed by this rise.

The reason is mine; no source is given. In a calm decline the selling is not forced. Underwater holders and slow reallocators let their coins go in pieces, and there is little leverage left to squeeze. An up day in such a market is usually a pause in that steady supply, made by a few buyers on thin volume, and the sellers resume.

A rise on heavy volume means something else. Some buyer has taken the whole day's supply and more. Because the market is calm, that buyer is unlikely to be a short forced to cover in a hurry. It is more likely a deliberate buyer: a value or informed holder, or an allocator who has decided the decline has gone far enough. Such buyers work large orders over several days rather than in one session. The first heavy day therefore shows demand that is not yet finished, and the price keeps rising as the rest of it arrives and as sellers who see the bid step back.

In an agitated fall the same heavy up day is more often a squeeze that is spent at once. That is why the effect is claimed for calm markets only.

The theory is wrong if, in falling, calm markets, heavy-volume up days are followed by no more rise than light-volume up days. It is also wrong if they are given back over the next days, or if heavier volume does not bring a larger later rise.
