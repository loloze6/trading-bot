In a falling, calm market, a coin that has fallen over three days keeps falling over the next three days. The size of the fall is measured against the coin's usual daily swing. The larger that scaled fall, the more of it continues.

The source gives no reason; it reports the pattern only. The reason that follows is mine.

In a calm downtrend there is no panic and no forced liquidation cascade. So a fall that is large for the coin's usual swing is unlikely to be an overshoot that liquidity providers are later paid to absorb. It is more likely an orderly sale by holders who have decided to leave. Such holders, and the funds that follow them, exit in pieces over several days rather than at once. The selling therefore spreads out, and later sellers react to the earlier fall.

The calm also matters to who waits on the other side. Dip-buyers who would absorb the fall in a rising market are absent or have stopped stepping in. Bargain hunters wait for a capitulation flush, and a calm market does not give them one. So the fall meets little patient demand and carries on.

The theory says this lives only in falling, calm markets. In agitated markets, the same scaled falls are more often forced selling that overshoots and is repaid.

The theory is wrong if, in falling, calm markets, scaled three-day falls are followed on average by a rebound or by no decline beyond the coin's usual drift. It is also wrong if larger scaled falls are not followed by more further decline than smaller ones.
