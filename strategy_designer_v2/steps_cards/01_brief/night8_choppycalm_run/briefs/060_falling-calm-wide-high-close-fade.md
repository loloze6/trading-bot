In a falling, calm market, a day whose range is wide against the coin's usual daily swing and that closes near its high is followed by a fall the next day. The fall is larger the wider the day and the nearer the close sits to the high. The source states the pattern but gives no reason, so the reason here is mine.

A coin that has drifted down for two months without unusual swings has a steady overhead supply. Holders bought higher, are underwater and are not forced out, and they sell into any strength. The calm says no forced deleveraging or news shock is driving prices. A wide day that ends at its high is then most likely a burst of urgent buying, such as short covering, one large order or chasing late in the day. That buying pushes through thin offers and pins the close high. It carries little new information, so it does not change the holders' wish to sell. The next day the burst is spent, the patient sellers return, and the liquidity providers who sold into the push are repaid as price gives the push back. A wider day stretches the overshoot further, so more of it is given back.

The theory is wrong if, in falling, calm markets, wide days closing near their high are followed by next-day returns no lower than usual. It is also wrong if that next-day weakness does not deepen with the day's width or with how close the close sits to the high.
