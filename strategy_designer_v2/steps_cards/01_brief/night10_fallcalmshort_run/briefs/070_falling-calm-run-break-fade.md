In a falling, calm market, a day that closes up after an unbroken run of down days tends to be followed by a fall the next day. The longer the run of down days before it, the larger that fall.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason below is mine. In a quiet downtrend nobody is forced to trade. There are no liquidation cascades and no short squeezes. The sellers are holders who are under water and still undecided. Each extra down day adds to the number of holders who want out but would rather not sell at the low. They are waiting for any relief. The first up day is that relief, and they sell into it.

Buyers in a calm decline are few and unhurried. They do not chase the bounce, so this patient supply pushes price back down the next day. A longer run leaves more of this waiting supply, so the giveback scales with the run's length. A rising market lacks this queue of trapped holders, so the effect should be absent there.

The theory is wrong if, in falling calm markets, up days after longer down runs are followed by next-day moves no weaker than those after shorter runs. It is also wrong if up days after runs of falls are, on the whole, followed by rises rather than falls.
