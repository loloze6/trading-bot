In a falling, agitated market, a coin that closes near the top of its day's high-low range tends to fall over the next day. The nearer the close sits to the day's high, the larger that next-day fall.

The source gives a short reason: the buying that lifts the close in such a market is short-lived. The rest of the reasoning is mine. In a coin that has been falling for weeks while swinging more than usual, buying late in the day is rarely new conviction. It is mostly short sellers covering as stops fire into a squeeze, and bargain hunters stepping in against the trend. Both kinds of demand are used up once they have acted. The supply behind the downtrend is still there: underwater holders waiting for any lift to sell, and levered positions still shrinking. When that supply meets a book with no buyers left to replace the covering, the strong close gives way.

In a calm falling market, closes are set by orderly flow, with little forced buying to fade. In a rising market, late buying is backed by real demand. So the effect should live where a downtrend and forced two-sided swings meet. This is the selling side of a close-location reversal: it rests on short-lived buyers, not on forced sellers.

The theory is wrong if, in falling agitated markets, closes near the day's high are followed by next days no weaker than closes in the middle of the range. It is also wrong if the effect is as strong in calm or rising markets as in falling agitated ones.
