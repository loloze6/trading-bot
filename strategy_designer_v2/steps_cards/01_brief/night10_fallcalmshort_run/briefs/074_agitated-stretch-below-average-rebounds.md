In an agitated market, the further a coin closes below its recent average, measured in units of its own usual daily swing, the more it rebounds over the following week or two. The effect is graded: a deeper stretch brings a larger rebound. This is the opposite of calm markets, where a stretch below the average tends to keep falling.

The source states the pattern and its contrast with calm markets, but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. When swings are larger than usual for the coin, a deep stretch below the average is mostly the work of sellers who must sell. These are holders whose position size is set by volatility and who cut as swings widen, levered longs being liquidated, and drawdown-limited holders selling in turn. Their selling carries no new view on the coin's value, so it pushes price past where patient buyers would set it. Those buyers are themselves held back by the same agitation and by their risk limits. They come back over days rather than at once, so the discount is repaid gradually. The deeper the stretch, the larger the forced share of the move and the larger the repayment. In calm markets, by contrast, a stretch comes from unhurried, informed selling that keeps working.

The theory is wrong if, in agitated markets, closes far below the average keep falling over the following days. It is also wrong if the later rise does not grow with the depth of the stretch, or if agitated and calm markets show the same behaviour after such stretches.
