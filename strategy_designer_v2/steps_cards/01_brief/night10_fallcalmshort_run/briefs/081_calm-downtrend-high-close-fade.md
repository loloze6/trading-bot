In a calm market, when a coin has been falling over the past few weeks, a day that closes in the upper part of its own range is followed by a fall the next day. The fall is larger the nearer the close sits to the day's high, and larger the deeper the few-week decline behind it.

The source states the pattern but gives no reason. The reason here is mine. A deeper few-week decline leaves more recent buyers holding at a loss. In a calm market these holders are not forced out by margin calls or stop cascades. They wait and sell into strength, often with resting orders above the price. A close near the day's high is buying that pushed into that layer of supply late in the session, before the sellers had filled. The next day the waiting supply meets the price, and the strong close is given back.

The effect should be weaker in agitated markets. There, strong closes are more often forced short covering or real turns, and larger two-sided swings blur the steady overhang of supply. In calm markets the buying behind a strong close is ordinary and unforced, so it has little to carry it past the overhang.

The theory is wrong if, in calm markets after a few-week decline, high closes are followed by next days no weaker than the coin's usual drift. It is also wrong if the next-day fall does not grow with the closeness of the close to the high, or with the depth of the decline. It is wrong too if the fall is as strong in agitated markets as in calm ones.
