In a choppy, calm market, a coin whose close sits near the top of its recent range tends to drift back toward the middle of that range over the following days. A coin closing near the bottom tends to drift back up. The pull grows the nearer the close is to an edge.

The reason is mine, not the source's. When a coin has neither trended nor swung more than usual for weeks, the traders who set its price are mostly patient ones: market makers holding inventory, range traders who sell near the recent high and buy near the recent low, and holders who take profit or add at the levels they remember. Nobody is forced to trade. There are few liquidations, few volatility-sized funds cutting exposure, and no trend for followers to chase. So a move toward an edge is mostly ordinary order flow rather than news, and the resting orders at the edges absorb it and push price back.

The effect should live in this profile only. In an agitated market, forced and information-led flow is strong enough to run through those resting orders, so edges break more often. In a rising or falling market, trend followers keep adding at the edge that lies in the trend's direction, so the pull back is weakened or reversed.

The theory is wrong if, in choppy calm markets, closes near the top of the recent range are followed by rises at least as large as closes near the middle. It is also wrong if closes near the bottom are followed by no rise. Finally, it is wrong if the pull back to the middle is no stronger in calm choppy markets than in agitated choppy ones.
