Idea: When a coin's 30-day volatility has risen above its 90-day volatility, a sharp one-day fall, measured against its 30-day typical daily swing, tends to be followed by a rise over the next day. The deeper the fall, the larger the rise.
Consequence: A trader can concentrate one-day rebound longs on days when agitation is rising, where hurried selling overshoots most, and refuse them when volatility is subsiding.
Why:
- F4: The rebound grows with the depth of the fall, and most where volatility has risen. On down days the slope of the next-day excess return on the scaled fall is -0.0070 (t -1.82, 3049 cases, 787 days). In the highest tercile of 30-day volatility against 90-day volatility the slope is -0.0195 (t -2.29, 684 cases, 278 days); in the lowest tercile it is +0.0045 (t 1.17). On up days the slope is flat (+0.0011, t 0.58).
- F5: In the choppiest tercile (30-day efficiency ratio), the same down-day slope is -0.0080 (t -2.60, 465 days), against -0.0067 (t -0.96) in the most trending tercile. The one-day rebound is therefore where the brief placed its reversal: choppy, agitated markets. It works on a one-day horizon, not a seven-day one.
- F2: A large one-day fall is followed by a one-day rebound. In the bottom decile of the daily return divided by its 30-day standard deviation, the next day's return minus the coin's mean daily return is +0.84 %. That is t 2.01 with the cases of one day taken together, on 627 cases over 204 days. The next decile is +0.38 % (t 1.29), and deciles 5 to 9 are all negative (-0.07 % to -0.44 %).
