A sharp price move that comes on unusually low volume tends to be partly given back, while a move of similar size on unusually high volume tends to hold or carry on. The reason is who is trading. When volume is thin, a move often reflects a few orders pushing through an order book that market makers have temporarily left: quiet hours, weekends, or a gap in liquidity. Such a move carries little new information. Once liquidity providers come back, they lean against the stretched price and earn the spread for doing so, so price drifts back. When volume is heavy, many participants are acting together, which suggests real news or a broad change in positioning. That kind of move is absorbed slowly: slower traders, funds that rebalance on a schedule, and trend followers keep adding in the same direction after the first burst. The reasons here are my own reasoning, not taken from any source. The theory is wrong if, after moves of similar size, later price behaviour does not differ by volume. It is also wrong if low-volume moves hold as well as high-volume ones, or if high-volume moves revert more.
