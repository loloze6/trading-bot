Idea: In a calm market, the further a coin's close sits below its 20-day average, measured in units of the standard deviation of its daily returns over the same 20 days, the more it keeps falling, over the next 5 days.
Consequence: A trader in a calm market shorts a deep stretch below the average for days rather than for two weeks, because the graded follow-through fades before ten days.
Why:
- F2: On the same negative-forecast cells the graded effect shows at shorter horizons. The slope of the drift-removed return on the forecast has t 2.67 at 3 days (738 cases) and t 2.58 at 5 days (435 cases), against 1.96 at 7 days and 0.22 at 10 days.
- F3: At 5 days the gain comes from the deepest stretches. The deepest quartile of the forecast (mean -18.2) is followed by an excess return of -3.3 %, the next quartile by -1.3 %, and the two shallower quartiles by +0.2 % and -0.6 %.
- F4: At 3 days the short side's drift-removed gain is +1.0 % per case (t 2.19 with the cases of one day together, 258 days). The slope is positive on all six coins (t from 0.25 to 1.78), so the short-horizon effect is not carried by one coin.
- F1: The test ran on all four folds, so no more data is available, and its 10-day slope sits at zero (t 0.29 on 445 negative-forecast cells). The same cells in folds B and C at 10 days give slope t 0.22 (215 cells), and the depth quartiles of the calm stretch are not ordered: excess 10-day returns of -1.7 %, -6.2 %, -4.4 % and -1.2 % from the deepest to the shallowest quartile (about 46 cases each).
